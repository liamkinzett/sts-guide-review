Teach effective reps as an explanation, not a law

The effective reps idea explains why the last reps of a hard set matter. Teach it as that. Never teach it as a law that every set must go to failure.

The reps in reserve targets in this topic are the prescription. The effective reps model (effective reps) is the explanation underneath them, not a separate instruction on top.

What the model explains. Muscle fibres are recruited in order of size (size principle). Early reps use the small, fatigue-resistant fibres. As those tire, the nervous system brings in the larger, higher-growth fibres to keep the weight moving.

Slowing bar speed under fatigue lets those fibres carry more of the tension.

This is why light and heavy loads taken to failure grow similar muscle. Heavy loads recruit the big fibres from rep one. Light loads need the early reps just to get there.

The same idea sits under rest-pause work and myo-reps, where short rests let her collect more of the hard reps for less total work.

Where the model misleads. Early reps are not wasted. They are the fatigue that makes the late-rep recruitment possible, so calling them ineffective misstates their job. The model also implies a switch, where a rep suddenly becomes effective past some RIR cutoff. Recruitment is continuous. Sets stopped several reps short still recruit the whole muscle over a block of training. Grinding every set to 0 RIR buys no extra recruitment, and stopping just short of failure grows the same muscle as strict failure.

How to teach it. Give her both halves. The mechanism, because it explains why a hard set feels the way it does and why stopping too early leaves growth on the table. The limit, because it does not licence taking every set to 0 RIR. The compound floor at 1 RIR still holds.

Why it works. A coach who only teaches the mechanism hands a client a licence to grind. A coach who only teaches the limit throws away a useful way to explain effort to a client who stops sets too early.

Watch for

A client who over-reads "effective reps" as licence to take every set to 0 RIR. She collects excess fatigue. On heavy compounds she adds injury risk from technique breaking down near failure. This is why the page is a judgement call rather than a firm rule.

Say it like this

The last few reps of a hard set do more of the work, and that's real. It doesn't mean every set has to be a grinder. I'll tell you how close to go.

Terms on this page

effective reps: The idea that the last few hard reps of a set, when the big motor units are working, do most of the growing. They are the reps before failure where the bar slows.

size principle: Motor units switch on from smallest to largest as the demand for force rises. The biggest fibres only join in when the small ones can no longer do the job.

RIR: Reps in reserve. How many more reps she could have done when the set stopped. Zero RIR is failure.
