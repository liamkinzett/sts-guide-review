The trap bar row and the supinated grip

Two things on this page. Why a lifter turns her palms up on a row, and what the trap bar row is for. Both come up when a coach writes the palms-up barbell row or the trap bar row into a program.

Turning the palms up on a row: two reasons, and they lead to different grips

Palms up is a supinated grip (supinated grip). There are two different reasons a coach reaches for it, and they do not point the same way. Say which one you are following when you prescribe the palms-up version.

Compared

columns: Take the rotators out of it | Buy lat length with the grip

The reasoning: The lat does not meaningfully rotate the arm in or out. Turning the palm up during the rep brings in the muscles that turn the upper arm outward (external rotators) and adds nothing for the lat. | A half-turned, palms-partly-up setting (semi-supinated) stops the upper arm sitting turned inward (internal rotation) and opens the chest. That lets her reach a longer stretched position of the lat.

What it prescribes: A neutral grip with the elbow travelling straight down to the hip. It is about turning the palm during the rep, and the same reasoning applies to the grip you choose. | A semi-supinated grip. It is an easier way in to a palms-up grip (supination) for a lifter whose upper arm does not turn outward far (external rotation).

Where it was coached: Cable and dumbbell rows. | A single-arm chest-supported machine row set semi-supinated, which STS doesn't program.

What is settled for the barbell. A barbell fixes the grip for the whole set, so the grip is a shoulder-position decision made once rather than a dial. The highest-intensity exercise keeps its one viable position, and variety is bought further down the session. Where the barbell row is the heavy row of the session, pick the grip her shoulder tolerates loaded and leave it there. Take grip variety on the cable and dumbbell rows instead.

A semi-supinated machine row is not a supinated barbell row

The semi-supinated setting in the second column was coached on a single-arm chest-supported machine row. STS programs full supination on a barbell. Those are not the same setup. Do not read one onto the other.

The trap bar row: what it is for

The trap bar row is reached by elimination. It is the heavy loadable row at the point where the rest of the family has run out of load. She has progressed through single arm dumbbell rows and then pull-ups, and there is no heavier row left. The purpose is systemic loading rather than isolating a muscle: a heavier loadable row, for power, not isolation. The plainest statement of the intent is a lot of load on her skeleton.

It is a poor choice for isolating the back. Do not select it as a hypertrophy row.

Why it sits at the end of the ladder. Complexity is the absence of external stability: more complex means less externally stable. The trap bar row is the loadable end of that ladder, not a new category of exercise.

On the trap bar row, the leg drive is the point

The pull is coached with deliberate knee and hip extension driving it. Everywhere else in this family that is the fault called body English. Here it is the movement, not leakage from it. Do not fault it. See Faults and what you can actually see.

The early limiter is coordination, not strength. The first sessions are limited by timing the knee and hip drive into the shoulder extension and the blade pulling in. Expect the load to climb as that sequencing improves.

Terms on this page

supinated grip: Palms facing up or toward her. On a barbell row it is the underhand grip, and it fixes the shoulder's rotation for the whole set.

external rotators: The muscles that turn the thigh outward at the hip, or the upper arm outward at the shoulder. At the shoulder they sit at the back of the joint.

semi-supinated: Palms turned half way up, between palms-down and palms-up.

internal rotation: Turning a limb inward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face back or down, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more toward the middle. An arm held that way is internally rotated.

supination: Turning the forearm so the palm faces up. A supinated grip is palms up.

external rotation: Turning a limb outward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face forward or up, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more to the side. An arm held that way is externally rotated.
