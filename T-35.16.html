Pain rules and when to refer · T-35.16

Cleared to train is not out of rehab

Two clients look identical on a message and are not. One has had a joint replaced and been discharged. The other is still in rehab and has been told she may train. This page separates them.

Where the line falls: frequency, not competence. The number one predictor of a rehab outcome is how often the clinician intervenes. An ACL reconstruction, a knee replacement, a rotator cuff repair: true post-operative rehabilitation is not deliverable as online coaching. The fix is co-management. Pair with a local hands-on professional.

Verdict

Still in rehab, even if cleared to train: co-manage with a local hands-on professional.

Post-operative rehabilitation is not delivered online.

For a client genuinely discharged, the script is not a prohibition. Never a bare no. Never alone. Four moves in one sentence: name the restriction, time-bound it, substitute something now, refuse to promise a date.

Say it like this

You've had a total knee replacement, so no, we can't do plyos right now. But let's do this set of exercises and build a base. If that's your goal, we'll work back towards it. I can't give you a specific time frame.

The standard behind it. Never say "you can't do X" without giving an alternative. And the counterweight: there might be things she quite literally can't and shouldn't do right now, and you accept that.

Two boundaries

This page does not license overriding a clinician. You may challenge a stale ban and never lift a medical restriction. A discharge is not a stale ban.

Programming a replaced joint comes from her clearance letter, not from a protocol. Read the letter behaviourally: which movements are restricted, which are cleared. Never a stage or a grade taken from the letter. Do not invent a load rule, a kneeling rule or an impact rule to fill the space.
