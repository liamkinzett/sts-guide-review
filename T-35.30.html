Pain rules and when to refer · T-35.30

When to call triple zero

Verdict

Call triple zero (000) for any of these. Do not ask her whether you should.

This is the threshold for an ambulance. It is a different decision from a referral, on a different clock.

The stop-and-refer table says when to send her to a clinician. This page says when to call an ambulance. They run on different clocks and the second never waits for the first. The list is the national health service's own, so you apply the same standard the operator will.

Red flags

title: Ask for an ambulance if someone has a medical problem such as

Loss of consciousness (blackout or fainting), even if brief

Chest pain

Sudden weakness in the face, arm or leg, or trouble speaking. These could be signs of a stroke

Breathing problems

A sudden collapse or unexplained fall

A seizure

Uncontrollable bleeding

Any other major concern about a person's health or wellbeing

emergency: Call triple zero (000) if you or another person is seriously injured or needs urgent medical help.

Chest pain has its own thresholds. It is the one most likely to be talked down by the person having it. Any of these is a triple zero call.

Red flags

title: Chest pain: any of these is a triple zero call

Chest pain that is severe, getting worse or has lasted longer than 10 minutes

Chest pain that feels heavy, crushing or tight

Chest pain with other symptoms, such as breathlessness, nausea, dizziness or a cold sweat

Chest pain and pain in her jaw or down her left arm

Palpitations (a racing heartbeat)

While you wait. Stop all activities and rest. Loosen any tight clothing, such as collar buttons. Do not give her anything to eat or drink.

You are not ruling anything out. Every row above is a symptom, not a diagnosis, and that is deliberate. You never name or grade a condition, and nothing here asks you to. You are reporting what you can see and hear to somebody whose job is to decide.

Say it like this

I'm calling an ambulance now.

Not "do you think we should call someone". The question invites her to talk you out of it. A client in this position very often will, because embarrassment arrives faster than symptoms do.

Why it works. The threshold is written by the body that answers the call, so you apply the same standard the operator will. The cost of being wrong runs one way. An ambulance that turns out not to have been needed costs an afternoon and some embarrassment.

Watch for

The talking-down. "It's probably just reflux", "I've had this before", "give me a minute". She does not have to agree with you. You are not asking permission to call, and the call is not a diagnosis you have to defend.

A knock to the head and an allergic reaction have their own pages, next in this topic. Each has its own signs and its own actions. They sit beside this list rather than inside it.
