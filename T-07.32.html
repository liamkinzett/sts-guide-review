A deload's scope follows its trigger

A deload's scope follows what triggered it. A local stall stays local. A systemic trigger takes the whole program down.

The deload pages so far say when a deload happens, how long it runs and what moves inside it. They do not say whether it covers the whole program or only the half that is beaten up. Two kinds of fatigue answer that differently.

Local fatigue stays local. The first trigger, regressing performance or volume, usually names specific lifts or muscles rather than the whole program. Fatigue from hypertrophy work does not reliably travel between distant, unrelated muscle groups. Training one muscle hard every day does not measurably drain recovery anywhere else. Where the trigger names a region, legs beaten up while everything else still moves, cut the sets on the affected muscles. Leave the rest of the program running at full dose.

"Unrelated" is doing real work in that sentence. Sessions that do not look connected can still be competing (crossover fatigue). Heavy hinging on a pull day can limit her bracing on a later hinge session. The cost shows up somewhere other than the muscle that paid for it.

The beaten-up half may share a synergist, a bracing demand or a joint position with a session elsewhere in the split. Then that session is not distant. It comes down with the half it is actually connected to. Split design has more.

Whole-program triggers stay whole-program. The other two triggers, life or illness and the week after a test day, are systemic by definition. Narrowing either of those to half the split misreads what forced the deload. The thing that caused it was never localised to a muscle group.

Check

Does the trigger name a specific lift or region, rather than her whole week?

Is that region unconnected to the rest of the split, with no shared synergist, bracing demand or joint position?

yes: Deload the affected muscles only. The rest of the program runs at full dose.

no: Deload the whole program. A stalling life does not name a muscle.

Terms on this page

crossover fatigue: Fatigue that shows up in a session other than the one that caused it. Heavy hinging on a pull day limiting her bracing on a later hinge session is an example.
