Spoto press

The Spoto press stops the bar just short of the chest, and it is right for exactly one fault: losing tightness on the way down. For the lifter who relaxes on the chest it is wrong, because that is a technique fault, not a strength fault.

Compared

columns: She loses tightness on the way down | She relaxes on the chest

What you see: The tightness is visibly gone before the bar reaches her chest | Her chest collapses at the bottom

Spoto press?: Yes. It is programmed strictly for the lifter who struggles to control the descent and loses tightness before touching | No. A technical fault, not a strength fault. Board and Spoto work reinforce the relaxation instead of removing it

What it gets her: She arrives at the chest in the position she left the top in | The feather-touch descent instead, which is technique

Execution. The bar stops short of the chest: a feather touch, or a paused hover an inch off the chest. Long pauses, moderate weight.

Who. In the strength system it is used only where she loses tightness on the way down. Where the fault is relaxing on the chest it is called useless.

The dumbbell version of the stop-short. For a lifter bouncing dumbbells off the chest, or whose chest collapses at the bottom: stop half an inch off the chest, pause, press. The same stop-short is the fix for a lifter with nagging shoulder pain or a flat chest.

The opposite client. A lifter who keeps flattening out rather than arching has the opposite fault, and no stop-short will help her. She cannot find a stacked rib cage lying back.

Cues

When: Setting up on the bench

Say: Top of the head to the ceiling, like you're pulling yourself up with a string.

Why: It stacks the rib cage she cannot find lying flat.

Alongside the cue, an externally supported low-incline press is the constraint. Gravity supplies the position she cannot find herself, until she can hold it.
