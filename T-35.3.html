Pain rules and when to refer · T-35.3

The other stop-and-refer triggers

Verdict

Any of these ends the session's plan and starts a referral conversation.

None is urgent the way cauda equina is. All of them change the plan.

None of these is an emergency. All of them end the session's plan and start a conversation. The trigger tells you to refer. The table below tells you to whom.

Red flags

title: Refer, and change the plan, when she has any of these

Joint pain plus any of swelling, mechanical locking, clicking or popping. The pain is the trigger and the sign qualifies it

On their own, with or without pain: the joint giving way, a sense that it will come out, or pain that wakes her at night

A suspected tendon problem where compression at the attachment is the aggravator (insertional tendinopathy), or a suspected acute tear

Any pelvic floor symptom, sciatica, postpartum bleeding or heaviness. Pain at the joint where the spine meets the pelvis (SI joint) or at the pubic symphysis

Low back: pain running down the leg (radicular pain), tingling, numbness or weakness

Any limb, arm or leg: tingling, numbness, a limb going dead, or weakness that is not fatigue

Late-stage joint disease (osteoarthritis) with severe mechanical symptoms

Persistent absent periods, or the other signs of too little food for her training (low energy availability). A medical hold, not a training decision

Anything acute: something went really wrong, really fast, so she gets checked out

Neck pain that is not from rowing and not from head position on a bench. No regional page exists for it: refer it on this table

emergency: Back or leg pain plus any cauda equina flag: emergency department the same day, not a GP appointment.

A suspected acute tear shows as blood where blood should not be. Bruising, swelling or heat after a moment she can name. It ends the session and is referred the day it happens. Region by region carries the regional versions, such as a sudden pop in the back of the leg.

A painless click is not a referral. On the joint row the pain is the trigger and the sign qualifies it. Instability and night pain are the exceptions: each is a referral in its own right. Neither is a load problem a coach can work around.

Tingling, numbness and weakness refer wherever they appear. Arm or leg, it is the same trigger applied consistently. It matters because the bucket method would otherwise sort a numb hand into the provocative bucket and queue it. A numb hand is not queued. It is referred. You route it and you do not characterise it.

Pregnancy and postpartum are coached at STS, so the routing matters more here than anywhere else in the table.

The practitioner types are the routing. A coach needs to know she is looking for a pelvic health physiotherapist rather than a general one. Which specific clinicians STS refers to is a business relationship, not a clinical fact, and it is not part of this topic.

What you never do on this table. Obtain a clinical grading, or palpate to find a diagnosis. Both are examinations a coach must never perform. What you act on is the presentation you can observe or be told about.

The neck. A head-position contraindication on an arched bench, with a cervical disc as its mechanism, lives in Region by region's neck section. Neck pain that is not rowing-related and not head-position related has no regional page: refer it on this table. The any-limb row above is the low back trigger applied to the arm as well. You route it and you do not characterise it.

Watch for

Referring and then continuing to program around the thing you referred for. Refer and change the plan.

Terms on this page

insertional tendinopathy: A tendon problem where the tendon attaches to bone. It is made worse when that attachment is compressed, such as at the bottom of a deep stretch.

SI joint: The sacroiliac joint, where the base of the spine meets the pelvis on each side, near the dimples of the low back.

radicular pain: Pain that runs down the leg along the path of a nerve, often with tingling, numbness or weakness. It means a nerve root is being irritated where it leaves the spine.

osteoarthritis: Wear of the joint surface that leaves the joint stiff, painful and mechanically rough. Late-stage disease with severe mechanical symptoms goes to a GP or sports physician.

low energy availability: Not enough food coming in to cover both her training and the rest of what her body needs to run. Persistent absent periods are its clearest sign.
