Her spine under load: what is actually true

Coaches are taught that a rounded spine under load is dangerous. The truth is more complicated, and a coach who repeats the simple version will cue things that are not real.

Three things are true at once.

  • Some flexion is present in every loaded lift, including the ones that look perfect. At 50% of body mass on her back the spine already flexes slightly in a squat. The change is too small for a coach to see. Lifts that look neutral involve some active rounding of the lower back.
  • The spine tolerates loads far beyond anything a client will meet. Compressive forces as high as 37,000 newtons have been measured in strength athletes without injury. Years of heavy squatting build bone density and spinal structure rather than wearing them down. Squat injuries trace to technique error, rapid volume jumps and repeated overload without recovery, not to the squat.
  • Risk sits where flexion at the outer range meets high compression AND forward shear. The compression runs down the length of the spine (axial load). The shear is the vertebrae sliding forward on one another. Deep forward flexion under a heavy bar raises the risk of disc injury and increases shear at the base of the lower back. The shear half is the part usually left out. Experienced lifters at 70% of their max adjust their spine in all three planes with little variation and never reach the outer ranges.

    • What this means for you. Her back is not fragile and a little movement in it is not a fault. What you are watching for is the very end of her range under a heavy bar. That is a gross loss of position, and it is visible. Diagnosing from video owns what to do when you see it.

    Watch for

    Cueing a neutral spine as though it were achievable and verifiable. It is neither. The movement screen already limits you to the gross loss of position you can actually see, and that limit is there for this reason.

    Say it like this

    Your back is not fragile. What we are avoiding is the very end of your range with a heavy bar on you, not the fact that it moves at all.

    Terms on this page

    axial load: Load running down the length of the spine, as with a barbell on the back or overhead.
