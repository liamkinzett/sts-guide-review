Bringing up a lagging muscle: move it before you add

A muscle that is not growing in step with the rest of her is brought up in three steps: move it, add, redistribute.

That muscle is a lagging muscle (lagging muscle). Climb them in order. Most coaches start at step two and skip the free one.

Climb it in this order

Move it. Program the muscle fresh rather than at the end of a session that has already buried it. Arms on a lower-body day. The lagging movement first in the session instead of fourth. A torso and limb split so it is never the tired part of a workout. This costs nothing in recovery and it is very often enough on its own.

Add, if the sets are cheap for the whole body. Take it to two or three sessions a week (exposures). Stack the volume on top of what it already receives indirectly. This is the right answer for arms and for small isolated muscles. Four to six extra sets on a client who is already pressing and pulling costs almost nothing overall.

Redistribute, if they are not. Hold a dominant, highly responsive muscle at the dose that keeps it where it is (maintenance volume). Do that for the whole block and spend what you free up on the lagging one. This is the answer for large muscles and for a client already at the top of her band. There, added sets carry real whole-body fatigue rather than local fatigue.

A muscle trained last is under-fresh, not under-dosed. Say it shares a day with the muscles that help it (synergists) and goes last. It has not been short-changed on sets. It has been short-changed on freshness. That is why step one comes first.

The line between steps two and three is a question about cost, not a list of muscles.

Do the added sets cost her recovery anywhere other than the muscle doing them? Apply that test and you can place a muscle this topic never mentions.

Training age moves the line. Step three exists because advanced lifters cannot recover from pushing every body part at once. An intermediate usually has the headroom to simply add. That is why she sits at the top of the volume curve in the classification table. An advanced lifter often does not, which is why she sits back down at ten to fifteen. Classify her first and the step chooses itself.

A worked example. An intermediate wants her shoulders to catch up. Step one: side raises move from last on push day to first. If two blocks later they are still behind, step two: shoulders go to a second day, four extra sets on a client who already presses. An advanced client at 20 glute sets and lagging hamstrings is a step-three case. Her glutes drop to a holding dose for the block and the freed sets go to hamstrings.

How many muscles at once. There is no set limit. One block might bring up glutes, hamstrings and shoulders together for a shared physique goal. Less volume goes to her back to pay for it. That is an example of the redistribution decision, not a rule. Ask whether her recovery can pay for each muscle you bias upward.

Watch for

Running step two on everything because it feels like coaching. Adding volume is visible work. Moving an exercise up the session is not. That is exactly why the free lever gets skipped.

Terms on this page

lagging muscle: A muscle that is growing more slowly than the rest of her on the same program. It is the target of a specialisation block.

exposures: The number of separate sessions in a week in which a muscle is trained. Two exposures means it is hit on two different days.

maintenance volume: The smallest dose that holds a muscle where it is without growing it. It frees sets for a muscle that needs them more.

synergists: Muscles that help the main muscle do a movement. Glutes and hamstrings are synergists in a hip hinge.
