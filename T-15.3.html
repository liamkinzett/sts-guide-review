Tempo and pauses · T-15.3

The pause goes where the muscle is loaded

Where a pause belongs is decided by the resistance profile and the bounce risk, never by range of motion. The one question is: at the moment you are asking her to hold, is the muscle actually loaded?

Sorting by range of motion is the wrong sort. Grouping movements into lengthened, shortened and mid-range and giving each a fixed pause looks tidy and puts pauses where nothing is happening. Range of motion tells you where the joint is. It does not tell you whether the muscle is carrying the load there. The way the load changes through the rep (resistance profile) does.

The three cases. A rep has a lowering half (eccentric) and a lifting half (concentric). The pause question is asked at the end of each.

  • Pause at the end of the eccentric wherever there is a loaded stretch or a bounce risk. A slight controlled pause at the bottom of a squat teaches her to hold tension rather than fall into it. A dead stop means the concentric starts from a position rather than from momentum. Bouncing transfers force off the muscle belly and onto tendon, ligament and joint.
  • The top of the repetition is usually a direction change, not a held squeeze. On most movements there is nothing to hold at the top, because tension has fallen away. The exception is a movement whose profile genuinely peaks in the shortened position. The pause table lists them, and the list is short on purpose. The one method that squeezes at the top of everything is ruled out on repetition length alone.
  • A well-cammed machine needs no terminal pause. A machine with a profile matched to the joint (cammed machine) holds tension through the entire range. On that equipment a terminal pause is solving a problem the machine has already solved.

    • Why it works. All three reduce to the same question. At the bottom of a Romanian deadlift the hamstrings are loaded, so the pause goes there. At the top of a barbell curl the biceps are not, so nothing is held. Range of motion cannot answer that question. The resistance profile can.

    Keep the muscle and the exercise apart. The bottom of an exercise is where the weight is lowest. The lengthened position of a muscle is where it is longest under load. On a squat they coincide. On a seated hamstring curl they are at opposite ends of the arc. The pause follows the muscle.

    Watch for

    A tempo copied from one exercise to another because they train the same muscle. A seated hamstring curl and a lying leg curl load the same muscle at opposite ends of its range.

    Terms on this page

    resistance profile: Where in the range an exercise is hardest and where it is easiest. It is set by the line of the load against the joint, so a dumbbell and a cable can load the same muscle very differently.

    cammed machine: A machine whose pulley is shaped so the load she feels changes through the rep. A well-designed cam matches how strong she is at each joint angle, so tension stays on the muscle throughout.

    lengthened position: The point in the rep where the working muscle is at its longest while it carries the load. It describes the muscle's length, not whether she feels a stretch.

    shortened position: The muscle is working while it is bunched short.

    eccentric: The lowering half of a rep, where the muscle lengthens under load.

    concentric: The lifting half of a rep, where the muscle shortens under load.
