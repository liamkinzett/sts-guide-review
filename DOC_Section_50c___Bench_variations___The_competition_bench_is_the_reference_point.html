The competition bench is the reference point

The competition-style bench is a separate movement, because the pauses make it one. On the strength path it carries its commands in the tempo: `21X1`. Two seconds down, one second on the chest, drive up as fast as she can, then one second locked out.

The bench has three commands: start, press, rack. The second pause is the start-command position, and that is the command lifters forget exists. The pauses go in from the first week of the strength phase. Added in the final weeks they are a new skill introduced at exactly the wrong time.

Who. It is the primary press for a powerlifting-goal client from the first week of the strength phase. For her the bench is one of the three lifts she is judged on, and non-negotiable. A physique-goal client has no reason to take the competition-style bench. At its hardest the tested lift holds one rep in reserve (RIR) and never crosses it. The lighter sets that climb toward the working weight (feeder sets) run one to eight reps on the tested lifts.

For a competitive powerlifter the competition bench is trained once a week, two to three sets. The second pressing day is a variation. Two to three sets a week counts exposures to the competition lift. The accessories carry the muscle volume.

Resistance is ascending: hardest at the bottom, easier as she locks out.

The bottom is where the leverage against her (moment arm) is longest. Tension peaks at the chest, in the lengthened position.

The setup standard every variation inherits.

Before the first rep of any barbell press

Shoulder blades pulled back and down before the unrack, and they do not move again. Retracted and depressed is the name for that position (scapulae). If she needs a mid-set cue, it is depression: blades down.

Elbows settle to roughly 45 degrees. Flared to 90 degrees of elbows-out (abduction) closes the space under the point of the shoulder (acromion). Over-tucked turns the lift into a triceps press.

Press back off the chest, not straight up.

Feet: at least 90 degrees at the knee, heels directly under it**. Behind it if she wants a tighter arch. Heels set outside that, then leg drive attempted, produces the hip or quad cramp (TFL) that locks the leg straight mid-set. It is visible on video before it happens.

On an arched bench the head stays down.

Session consequences that travel with every barbell variation. The barbell press pins the shoulder blade. An overhead press or a pulldown later in the same session restores its outward turn (upward rotation). Heavy lat rowing immediately before pressing tires the muscles that hold the shoulder blade still (scapular stability), and pressing performance drops.

Terms on this page

scapular stability: The shoulder blade held still on the rib cage while the arm works.

RIR: Reps in reserve. How many more reps she could have done when the set stopped.

feeder sets: Light sets done before the working sets to rehearse the movement and find the day's load. They tune the pattern and the load without adding fatigue.

lengthened position: The point in the rep where the working muscle is at its longest while it carries the load. It describes the muscle's length, not whether she feels a stretch.

moment arm: The perpendicular distance between the joint axis and the line of force.

scapulae: The two shoulder blades. One is a scapula. Retracted means pulled together; depressed means pulled down.

abduction: A limb travelling out and away from the midline of the body: the arm in a lateral raise, or the thigh moving out to the side. On the bench, how far her elbows sit from her body.

acromion: The bony roof at the top of the shoulder blade that sits over the shoulder joint. The tendons of the shoulder run underneath it.

TFL: The tensor fasciae latae, a small muscle at the front and side of the hip that runs into the band down the outer thigh. It cramps when she drives with her legs from a bad foot position.

upward rotation: The shoulder blade turning so its outer corner rises and the socket tilts upward. It is what lets the arm travel overhead.
