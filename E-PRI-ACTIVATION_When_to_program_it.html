When to use it

The problem you notice. There is one position she loses every time in the main lift, and you know which one before the session starts. Or she cannot feel a muscle at all. Or she cannot yet pull, and hangs with her shoulders at her ears. An activation drill rehearses the position or wakes the muscle while it is still cheap, then demands it back under load.

Warming up decides whether any of this goes in. These rows say which drill once it does. Priming here means prep work, not the readiness phase (prep work).

Reach for it when

# There is one position she loses every time in the main lift

sub: You already know which one before the session starts

Confirm it: Her last three videos of the main lift show the same position going. Say which.

What it gets her: The position rehearsed while it is still cheap, then demanded back under load. A position she often struggles with goes into the warm-up or an early exercise in the day, so she can call back on that position. The warm-up and the working set are placed close together on purpose.

# She cannot feel a muscle group at all

sub: It reads flat, and it stays flat under load because she never finds it

Confirm it: Ask her where she felt the last set. If the target muscle is never the answer, she has not found it.

What it gets her: The mind-muscle connection woken before the muscle is loaded (mind-muscle connection). She contracts the muscle deliberately, unloaded, in its fully shortened position. That is strategic posing, and it is for flat or under-felt muscle groups.

# She cannot yet pull

sub: She hangs with her shoulders at her ears and the arm does all of it

Confirm it: Watch her hang. The shoulder blades never move down or forward before the arm bends.

What it gets her: The shoulder blade pulled down (depression), slid forward (protraction) and turned up (upward rotation) before the arm is asked to pull. The shoulder-blade drills, the scap tier (scapula), are the beginner rung of a pull-up progression. Scapula dip, scapula push-up and serratus rollout on a wall, with the scapula pull-up above them. The hanging scap raise is STS's form of the scapula pull-up. Pull-up has the whole ladder.

# The session is short and a drill would cost you a working set

sub: Prep and stimulus from the same movement

Confirm it: Count the slots. There is no room for a drill that does not also count.

What it gets her: The half-kneeling landmine press doubles as warm-up and first working exercise (landmine). That is preferable, because drill work is not really going to be high-stimulus stuff.

# Her shoulder objects to pressing straight overhead

sub: The landmine as a rung on the overhead ladder

Confirm it: A seated dumbbell overhead press provokes the shoulder, and a machine or Smith press was the rung before it.

What it gets her: A press angle that comes off vertical, which is usually what the shoulder objects to. The kneeling landmine press is the rung below the seated dumbbell press on that ladder, with the front raise below it. Shoulders owns the ladder.

The commonest reason to reach for a drill is the one STS rejects

Rotator cuff isolation is the standard corrective prescription, and it usually misses. Often it is not a weak rotator cuff or a weak serratus that is the issue. These muscles are just in bad positions to produce force. Fix the position and the movement before you prescribe the isolation. A drill programmed because something looked weak is that failure.

A lengthening warm-up is a programming diagnostic, not a warm-up problem. When the warm-up starts collecting kettlebells, a lacrosse ball, a dowel and a band, those are indicators that something is missing in the program. Find the loadable version of each corrective and move it into her accessory work. Do not add another drill.

One contraindication, and it is a test mistaken for a treatment. The kettlebell bottoms-up press, which STS doesn't program. If someone's shoulder is already bugging, this is not the way to make it feel better.

Setup. Half-kneeling landmine by default, leg under the base of support, scaps set before load. Setup has the full list.

Terms on this page

prep work: The movements inside the warm-up slot that get the first loaded movement ready. Chosen for the session in front of you.

mind-muscle connection: Her ability to feel a muscle working and deliberately contract it during a rep.

depression: The shoulder blade pulled down, away from the ear. The opposite of a shrug.

protraction: The shoulder blade sliding forward and out around the rib cage, away from the spine. The opposite of pinching the blades together.

upward rotation: The shoulder blade turning so its outer corner rises and the socket tilts upward. It is what lets the arm travel overhead. A bench press holds it in the opposite position.

landmine: A barbell with one end anchored to the floor or in a sleeve, so the free end moves in an arc.

scapula: The shoulder blade. The flat bone on the back of the rib cage that the arm hangs from. "Scap" is the gym short form, so a scap raise or a scapula dip moves the shoulder blade on its own before the arm joins in.
