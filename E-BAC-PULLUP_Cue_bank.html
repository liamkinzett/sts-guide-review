Cues

Cues go in the order printed. Try the first, and only move on when it fails. Most of the named faults are failures of the bottom position rather than of the pull, which is why the position cues come first.

Cues

When: In the hang, before anything moves

Say: pulling your shoulder blades into your pockets

Why: Internal cue. Sets lat tension in the hang before the pull starts.

When: The start of the pull

Say: driving your elbows down toward your pockets

Why: Internal cue. Starts the pull from the lat rather than from the arm.

When: A fingertip or thumb-over grip

Say: knuckles above the bar

Why: External cue. Turns the grip into a position you can check, rather than an instruction to try harder.

When: She swings

Say: gymnast pull-up

Why: External cue. The whole-body standard in one image. Use it when the fault is swing rather than position.

When: The gymnast image does not land

Say: the feet never come behind the body

Why: External cue. The rule underneath the gymnast image. It is checkable from the side in a single frame, which the image is not.

When: Her feet merely dangle

Say: feet actively in front of your body

Why: External cue. The same standard stated as something to do rather than something to avoid.

When: The external phrasings have not worked

Say: Contract your quads

Why: Internal cue. The front thigh muscle (rectus femoris) is a strong hip flexor. Contracting it bends the hip and holds the pelvic position the three cues above describe from outside. Cueing explains why internal cues come after external ones.

Terms on this page

rectus femoris: The one quad muscle that crosses the hip as well as the knee, running down the front of the thigh. Contracting it flexes the hip, which is why "contract your quads" holds her legs in front of her.
