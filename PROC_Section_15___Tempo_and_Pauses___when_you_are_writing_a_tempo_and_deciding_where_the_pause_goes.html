Writing a tempo and deciding where the pause goes

This page is the whole topic in the order you use it. Two questions arrive together: whether a tempo needs writing at all, and where the pause belongs. The second has no universal answer. It is decided per movement, and this page ends at the pause table rather than at a default code.

Test. Ask what the tempo is for before you write one. Tempo is an execution variable, not a growth variable. It decides whether the exercise you selected is the exercise she performs.

It is also part of the context you hold still for the logbook, which is why the answer to "do I write one" is yes. If you are reaching for it because she stopped progressing, stop. Volume owns a stall, and tempo is not on its list.

Then the question that decides the pause: at the moment you would ask her to hold, is the muscle actually loaded? Range of motion cannot answer that. The way the load changes through the rep (resistance profile) can.

Read. The answer is written per movement, and it sorts three ways.

Three cases

# Case 1. A loaded stretch, a bounce risk, or both

sub: The pause goes at the end of the eccentric

Examples: Squat; Romanian deadlift; Bench press; Seated hamstring curl; Lat pulldown

# Case 2. A matched machine profile holding tension throughout

sub: No pause. There is nothing for a pause to add

Examples: Cable work through a continuous arc; Well-cammed machines

# Case 3. A profile that genuinely peaks in the shortened position

sub: The pause goes at the end of the concentric. A short list on purpose

Examples: Hip thrust; Leg extension; 45 degree hip extension

Never copy a tempo between exercises because they train the same muscle. A seated hamstring curl and a lying leg curl load the same muscle at opposite ends of its range. And read the code itself correctly. Positions 2 and 4 are defined by the phase, not by where the weight is in space. On a seated curl the end of the eccentric is knees extended with the pad up. It is not the bottom.

Do. Look the movement up in the pause table and take the code from its row. A movement that is not listed is matched to the nearest row by asking where the muscle is actually loaded.

Around the pause the digits are settled. Eccentric two to three seconds, three to four while she is learning it, never past four, with five for a very tall client. Concentric one second, never deliberately slow. X, meaning maximal intent, on squat, bench and deadlift for a client training for strength.

Write the pause by phase: write "pause in the stretch" or write the number. Never write "pause at the bottom". Then the exceptions.

Change. This step is mostly what does NOT change. Tempo is part of the context you hold still, and it is not a progression lever.

It holds through an intensifier, a technique such as a drop set that adds work to the end of a set. It holds through a planned easier week (deload). If she cannot hold the tempo through the drop, the drop is too heavy.

This topic makes two scale-backs. In a deep deficit, eating well below what she burns (deficit), the held pause comes off the heaviest stretch-loaded movements. It stays on lighter work where the machine holds her position (externally stabilised). A stretch-paused movement never opens a cold session.

When you do change a tempo knowingly, two things move with it. The logbook comparison ends, and her reps in reserve (RIR) stop meaning what they meant. Expect the logged load to fall. Do not read the fall as a stall.

Terms on this page

resistance profile: Where in the range an exercise is hardest and where it is easiest. It is set by the line of the load against the joint, so a dumbbell and a cable can load the same muscle very differently.

eccentric: The lowering half of a rep, where the muscle lengthens under load.

concentric: The lifting half of a rep, where the muscle shortens under load.

shortened position: The muscle is working while it is bunched short.

RIR: Reps in reserve. How many more reps she could have done when the set stopped.

deload: A planned easier week that lets fatigue clear while the muscle and the skill stay.

deficit: Eating fewer calories than she burns, so that she loses body fat.

externally stabilised: The machine, bench or support holds her position so she does not have to.

tendinopathy: A tendon that has become painful and irritable from more load than it has adapted to. It is diagnosed by a clinician, not by a coach.

isometric: A hold. The muscle works against the load but the joint does not move.
