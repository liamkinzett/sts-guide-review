Does explosive work like jumps build muscle size?

How fast force can be produced is a separate trainable quality from how much force can be produced. Jumps and other explosive work build it. Its name is rate of force development (rate of force development). By default it does not earn a place in a program whose goal is to look better.

Explosive work trains the ability to produce force quickly. That is a valuable athletic quality. It is not automatically a high-priority tool for building muscle.

Maximal strength is the ceiling on force. Rate of force development is how fast that force arrives, typically inside about 200 milliseconds, far too short for maximal force to ever be reached. The two come apart in training:

  • chasing bar speed with few reps and little slowing (velocity-based training) builds explosive power, top bar speed and jump height;
  • fixed loads with more reps and more slowing build average power and total work.

    • Same starting lifters, different results.

    Explosive strength is also the first thing fatigue takes. It depends on rapid recruitment of the fastest fibres (Type IIx). Those are the first compromised as by-products build up.

    Why it matters. Specificity asks what quality an exercise builds before what it resembles. Jumps build rate of force development. That quality does not reliably carry over to size. So against a look-better goal, explosive work is not earning its place.

    When to leave the default. Keep it in where there is another reason: athleticism, sport carryover, or a lifter simply enjoys it. Name the reason. It is not earning its place on the size argument alone.

    A worked case. A client whose goal is size loves box jumps. Keep them because she enjoys them, and say so. Put them early in the session, before she is fatigued. Do not count them toward her size work.

    Watch for

    Explosive work late in a session, when the muscles are already fatigued. The fastest fibres fail first under fatigue. Jumps at the end of a session train the wrong quality for what the body can currently produce.

    Say it like this

    Jumps build a different quality to the one you're actually after. They're not bad for you. They're just not the best use of your time if the goal is size.

    Terms on this page

    rate of force development: How fast a muscle can produce force, not how much. It matters for jumping and sprinting more than for size.

    velocity-based training: Training that picks reps and loads by bar speed, stopping a set when the bar slows past a set point.

    Type IIx: The fastest, most explosive muscle fibres. They tire quickest, and with regular use they take on a more fatigue-resistant character.
