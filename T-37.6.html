Returning to training · T-37.6

Detraining is not one process: sort the return by its length

Different adaptations leave on different clocks, so losing training is not one process and a return is not one protocol. The first thing to sort is how long she was away, and why.

The return, by length of layoff

Two weeks off or less, no injury. The program picks up unchanged. No easing back in. Warn her the same load will feel heavier for a session or two.

Longer than two weeks. A layoff. Run the layoff return: compound work, few movements, at a lower percentage than she finished on. Never give her a week count of what she lost. Give her the protocol.

At three weeks, what she sees in the mirror is mostly not muscle loss. Fluid, glycogen and the pump are gone. The tissue largely is not. Two to three weeks is the window before genuine detraining starts, so say this before she looks.

Injury or illness. An injured tissue runs the ladder on the injury pages, whatever the calendar says. Illness: training stops while she is sick, and her doctor decides when she is well. The way back is a ramp on weight over two to three weeks. The illness pages have it.

Two weeks is the scope of "pick up unchanged". Someone who takes two weeks off strength training and says she has to ease back in does not. This rule exists to stop a coach deloading a client who does not need it. The layoff return owns anything longer, and the two do not blur.

A deload week is not a layoff either. After a planned easier week (deload), loads pick up where they were. No reset and no ramp back in. Ramping back in treats a week off as detraining and costs her time re-climbing ground she never lost.

Illness is not covered by the two-week rule. That rule comes from a person who simply stopped training. A client coming back from being unwell does not pick up where she left off. Training stops while she is sick. Her doctor decides when she is well, and the way back is a ramp on weight over two to three weeks. The illness pages later in this topic have the detail.

One thing will be true and she should be warned. The same load genuinely feels heavier after a layoff. The protection a muscle builds against work it has done before (repeated bout effect) fades in perception as well as in tissue. Told in advance, it is expected. Discovered under the bar, it reads as lost strength.

Say before her first session back

The same weight will feel harder this week. That's normal, and it isn't lost strength.

Terms on this page

deload: A planned easier week that lets fatigue clear while the muscle and the skill stay. After it, loads pick up where they were.

repeated bout effect: The protection a muscle builds against the soreness and damage of work it has done before: the next session of the same movement produces far less of both. It fades with time off, so the same load feels harder after a layoff.
