Fatigue and recovery · T-03.11

Crossover fatigue between sessions

When her bench stalls in a week of heavy squatting, two reads fit, and which one you reach for depends on her training history.

Start with what you can rule out. In most lifting sessions the muscles already hold enough stored carbohydrate (glycogen), so fuel is rarely the answer.

The pattern itself has a name. Fatigue generated in one session that shows up as underperformance in a later one is (crossover fatigue). It is invisible in the session that suffers, because the cause sits a day or two earlier. If a day underperforms consistently, look at what precedes it before you look at what is in it.

The two reads sit either side of a real split. Tiredness that comes from the nervous system and affects the whole body is (central fatigue). Tiredness that sits in the muscle itself and the point where the nerve meets it is peripheral fatigue (peripheral fatigue). A blanket squat-then-bench complaint could be either.

Compared

columns: Central fatigue | Peripheral fatigue

The read: Heavy squatting in 20 rep sets damages muscle. That damage feeds back to tire the whole nervous system. Her bench pays for her squats | The clients who complain of this are usually the ones who have never trained real volume. What they feel is fatigue local to the muscle and its nerve junction (neuromuscular junction), and it fades as they adapt

What you do: Space the heavy leg volume away from the lift you are trying to progress | Build her volume tolerance gradually and let the feeling pass

Why this is your call, not a settled fact. The two reads are not fully reconciled in the source material behind this rule. Across different discussions, the same reasoning moves between treating it as a central-fatigue effect and treating it as plain inexperience. One source names the mechanism itself as something coaching has not properly pinned down, rather than choosing between the two.

Nobody has a test that separates the two in the gym. What is left for you is training history. A client with years of volume behind her earns the first read. A client new to real volume usually earns the second. The two look identical in the logbook, so the call is yours.

The one settled point. Fuel is usually not the limiter in resistance work. Lifters lean too hard on pre-training carbohydrate. Anything about what she eats belongs to the Nutrition Manual.

Terms on this page

glycogen: Carbohydrate stored in the muscle and liver. It is the fuel hard training runs on.

crossover fatigue: Fatigue that shows up in a session other than the one that caused it. It is invisible in the session it hits, because the cause sits a day or two earlier.

central fatigue: Tiredness in the nervous system and the whole body, not in one muscle. The signal to the muscle drops, so every lift pays.

peripheral fatigue: Tiredness that sits in the muscle itself, or at the point where the nerve meets it, rather than in the central nervous system. It fades as the muscle adapts to the work.

neuromuscular junction: The point where a nerve meets a muscle fibre and passes on the signal to contract.
