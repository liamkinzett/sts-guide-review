Muscle and growth · T-01.2

Does a big response right after training mean more long-term growth?

What a session sparks in the next few hours does not predict how much growth follows. Never use it to justify a protocol.

It is tempting to judge a training method by what happens right after one workout. You can measure things like muscle protein synthesis, electrical activity, swelling and signalling molecules within hours. Muscle growth takes weeks and months to measure. That gap creates an obvious temptation: use the short-term response as a preview of the long-term result.

The problem is that the two do not reliably line up. A bigger short-term rise in muscle protein synthesis (muscle protein synthesis) after one session does not mean more growth follows. It does not predict which protocol will build more muscle over the following months.

Coaches call that short-term rise an acute response (acute response): a change measured during or soon after one session, not the growth itself. When acute response and long-term growth have been checked against each other, they come apart. The session with the biggest immediate response has not consistently been the one that grew the most muscle.

This matters because a big short-term response gets used to justify protocols that have not actually shown a bigger long-term result. That includes an argument built on a larger post-workout rise in protein building. It includes a bigger pump, more soreness, greater "activation", or a bigger short-term signalling response. It includes most arguments about an "anabolic window", and most arguments for a particular post-session meal time.

These measurements can tell you something about what happened during or right after the session. They should not automatically be treated as measurements of future growth.

Why the shortcut is tempting. Protein building can be measured in hours. Growth takes months. That makes the short measure look like a handy preview of the long one. It is not.

What this means for coaching. Judge a hypertrophy program by what happens across the whole training block. Do not judge it by which single workout produced the most dramatic short-term response. Look at her training performance, her progression, her measurements and how she looks over months. A useful rule: what a session sparks is not the same thing as what a block builds.

Watch for

The same argument in a different costume. Activation, pump, soreness, "metabolic stress". Same shape, same problem. What a session feels like, or sparks, is not an outcome.

Say it like this

I don't program around what one session does in the next few hours. I program around what a block does over months.

Terms on this page

acute response: A short-term change measured during or soon after a training session.

muscle protein synthesis: The process that builds new muscle protein after training. It rises for a day or more, then returns to normal.
