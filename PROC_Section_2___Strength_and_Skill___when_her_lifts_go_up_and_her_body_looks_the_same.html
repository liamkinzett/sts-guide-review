What to tell a client whose lifts are climbing without a visible change

She is lifting more every week and says she looks exactly the same. This page walks Strength and skill in the order you use it, inside that conversation.

Test. Read the client, not the calendar.

Three things to read first

Where does she sit between adaptation and optimisation? Her training age, her logbook, and whether the same effort still buys big jumps. You are reading a client, not a category.

Do not date it. Several week counts for the "neural phase" circulate. STS uses none of them. Never tell her a date it ends.

What is the program meant to build? If the adaptation being trained is strength, this is the program working. If the goal is a visible change, ask whether her exercises train that adaptation, not what they resemble.

Read. These systems adapt on different timescales. They overlap rather than happening as separate stages: nervous system and skill first, then muscle, then tendon and ligament, then bone.

The nervous system adapts fastest. The fast-adapting tissue is doing the improving and the slower-adapting tissue has not caught up yet. Her lifts are climbing on stability, skill and neural strength. The early weeks are a skill problem wearing a strength problem's clothes.

Read the risk in the same picture. Force-production capacity can improve faster than some slower-adapting tissues are prepared to tolerate.

Do.

In this order

Tell her in advance. The first stretch moves quickly because the nervous system learns fast. Tendons and bones take much longer, so we build the habit now and the load later.

Do not give her a week count for when it ends. A number implies a date. A coach who has that date will blame a stall on it. The mechanism is real and the timetable is invented.

Keep a beginner on pattern rather than load. That is why Her first program starts her there.

Say now that it will stop. Fast early progress is not talent and not a well-designed program. It is the nervous system, and it is temporary.

Change. Nothing, on this alone. The presentation is progress, not a problem. Where the goal is a visible change, run the specificity question over her exercises: how closely does each one train the adaptation she wants. Dose questions belong to Volume, Effort and intensity and Progression.

What explicitly does not change. The program is not swapped because the mirror lags the bar. She is not declared "out of her newbie gains" on a date.

What not to promise her

Never give her a week count for the early strength phase. Tell her strength comes first and size follows, without a date.

Never promise when the visible change arrives. Point her at what she can see now: her numbers going up.

Never prescribe extra practice to speed it up. Practising a lift covers how she learns a movement.

Treat the pattern as a starting point for her. Her own logbook confirms it.
