Pain rules and when to refer · T-35.14

A years-old restriction is interrogated, never inherited

"Someone told you you can't" is not a reason. Ask which of two things the old ban is: a structural limitation, or an old instruction.

The whole thing sits on one question. She says she can't squat because she hurt her knee four years ago, or can't press because of a shoulder surgery 10 years ago. Why can't she? Because she hasn't tried? Because someone told her she can't? What is the real reason: does she have structural damage or a structural limitation? The genuine exception is real structure. A shoulder with 13 pins in it is a limitation.

A standing ban can do harm on its own

A clinician who tells her to do single-leg work and never squat again may have hurt her with that sentence.

Once the tissue has healed, what is left is the nervous system, and the nervous system can change instantly.

Continuing to avoid the movement is part of the problem, not protection. "I've been avoiding bench for a few months now" is often the problem itself.

The clock is the wrong instrument. "This from weeks one to four and this from four to six" on a photocopied sheet tells you nothing about her. Lose the idea of time and set up performance landmarks instead.

A ban's age tells you nothing in either direction. Old does not mean stale. Old does not mean valid. Present capacity is the only thing that answers it. Returning to training explains how: command before demand.

Why a ban outlives its evidence. "Squats are bad for your knees" dates from 1961, was wrong, and is still repeated in gyms today. A ban does not expire on its own. Somebody has to ask the question.
