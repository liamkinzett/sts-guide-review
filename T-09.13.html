Program percentages off an estimated one rep max

An estimated one rep max is a live number you program from and keep correcting.

The estimate (e1RM) is a projected maximum worked out from reps she completed at a lighter load. It is not a statistic you file, and not something you wait to replace with a tested max.

What it buys. Percentage work becomes available to a client who is nowhere near ready to test a true maximum. You take a percentage off the estimate to set the day's working weight. She never has to attempt a single she cannot safely attempt.

Why it can beat a tested maximum. A beginner adapts fast enough that a real tested number goes stale within weeks. Early in a peaking block her tested max is already the wrong number. The only way to correct that is to test again. An estimate does not have that problem. You move it.

Where the first number comes from.

Setting her first estimate

Test a heavy triple, not a five. Take the heaviest clean triple. If the third rep is a grind, that is the set. She does not chase another.

Read the estimate as the average of the Epley and Brzycki equations at three reps.

Keep the reason it is a triple. Epley overestimates a max from five-rep loads and predicts accurately from three. A five-rep test inflates every percentage that comes off it for the rest of the block.

A client who cannot yet safely test a heavy triple has no verified number. Keep her in reps in reserve until she does.

It is maintained, not calculated once. Adjust it across the block on how her working sets actually look and feel. If the prescribed percentage lands far from the intended effort, the estimate is wrong, and the estimate is what you change. That is the whole advantage of the method. It is lost if you set the number in week one and leave it there.

What makes you move it. Not a size of change. The trigger is a mismatch between the percentage you prescribed and the effort it produced. Sets at 80% moving like sets at 90% mean the estimate is too high. Sets at 80% moving like sets at 70% mean it is too low. Effort is something you can see on a video and read in a logbook.

Where it is soft. Every projection is a formula, and its error grows with the number of reps it was projected from. A projection from a set of twelve is much softer than one from a set of four. Estimate from lower rep work where the lift allows it. Treat a change of a few percent as noise. Two estimates from different rep counts are not really comparable, even for the same lifter on the same lift.

She sees the number, and she is told it is theoretical. Three reasons, and the first matters most.

  • It teaches her to work in percentages, so she stops needing a coach to hand her a working weight every week.
  • It gives her a real target to match or beat when a test day arrives.
  • It is fair. She is using the number. She should know what it is.

    • Why this is the opposite call to her body fat figure, which she never gets. The difference is what the number is for. A body fat estimate is a verdict about her body carrying a precision the method cannot support. An estimated maximum is an instrument she operates. It is labelled theoretical when she receives it, and it gets more useful the more she understands it. Assessing a new client has the body fat rule.

    Say it like this

    Your estimated max is a working number, not a test result. We use it to set your percentages. If your sets start moving faster or slower than they should, we change the number, not you.

    Watch for

    Letting it drift upward because it is flattering. The estimate exists to produce the right working weight. If her sets at 80% are moving like sets at 90%, the number is wrong in the direction nobody enjoys correcting.

    Terms on this page

    e1RM: Estimated one rep max. The heaviest single she could probably lift, projected from a set of several reps at a lighter weight.
