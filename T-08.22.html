Active insufficiency: why a bent knee takes the hamstring out

A muscle shortened at both of its joints can barely produce force.

That is active insufficiency (active insufficiency). It is the mechanism behind counting the hip thrust as glutes only.

Two places it shows up in our exercises.

  • The hamstring in a hip thrust, with the knee bent.
  • The hamstring in a seated leg curl at full knee lockout. That is why the leg curl page tells you not to lock the knees out under load.

    • Why it works. The hamstring crosses the hip and the knee. In a hip thrust the knee stays bent, which shortens it at one end. As she drives up, the hip extends, which shortens it at the other. Shortened at both ends, it has almost no room left to pull. The glutes have to do the extension instead.

    One term, used precisely. Passive insufficiency is a different idea. Do not define it as the opposite of this one, and do not use it to explain a counting decision. The calf raise page answers why a bent-knee calf raise is read as soleus work. Read it there.

    Terms on this page

    active insufficiency: A muscle that crosses two joints and is shortened at both is too bunched up to produce much force. Bending the knee does this to the hamstrings in a hip thrust.
