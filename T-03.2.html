Fatigue and recovery · T-03.2

Does soreness mean the session worked?

Soreness does not tell you a session worked. Its absence does not tell you one failed. Read the logbook instead.

Next-day soreness (DOMS) and the damage marker in the blood (creatine kinase) do not track muscle growth. Muscle damage itself is not a reliable sign that muscle is being built.

Why it works. Soreness is the most available feedback a client has. It arrives on a convenient timetable. It is also the feedback most likely to come from novelty rather than from a good stimulus. A new exercise reliably produces it. A productive one often does not.

What goes wrong if you use it as the scorecard. Treat soreness as the main marker of a good session and this follows:

  • a genuinely useful session gets undervalued because she was not sore;
  • a poor or excessive one gets praised because it hurt;
  • and plain exercise novelty gets mistaken for good programming.

    • What to watch instead.

  • performance trends;
  • the quality of the work she is doing;
  • visible or measurable adaptation;
  • how well she is recovering;
  • and whether the current program can be repeated and progressed.

    • Those tell you far more than how she feels the next morning.

    Watch for

    A client changing her own program to chase soreness. She swaps a movement that was working for one that hurts.

    A coach doing the same thing. It shows up as rotating exercises for their own sake. Choosing exercises covers when a swap is earned.

    Say it like this

    Being sore doesn't tell us the session worked, and not being sore doesn't tell us it didn't. The logbook tells us.

    Terms on this page

    doms: Delayed onset muscle soreness. The soreness that arrives a day or two after training and fades over the next few days.

    creatine kinase: A protein that leaks from muscle into the blood when the muscle membrane is damaged. It marks damage, not growth.
