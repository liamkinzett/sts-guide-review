Diagnose a breaking pattern before you change its frequency

When a pattern is breaking down, the instinct is to program it more often so she gets more practice. That is right about half the time and it makes the other half worse. Ask what is causing the breakdown before you touch frequency.

Three causes that look the same on video

# Skill. She has not learned it yet

sub: Low training age, pattern clean when fresh, no better across weeks

What you see: The breakdown is there on the first rep of the first set, and it does not improve as the weeks pass.

Do this: Raise the pattern's frequency. More exposures, low fatigue.

# Fatigue. She knows it and cannot express it

sub: Clean early in a session and falling apart later, or degrading as the block goes on

What you see: The pattern is fine on set one and worse on set four. Or it was fine in week one and worse in week four.

Do this: Reduce frequency or load and let the fatigue clear.

# Load. She cannot yet hold position at a high absolute load

sub: Perfect until the bar gets heavy, whatever her reps in reserve

What you see: Once the load is high, the breakdown arrives on the first rep of the first set. Read against the two rows above she looks like a skill problem. She is not one. This is not permanent. It is true of her now.

Do this: Cap the absolute load. More frequency does not supply that lever. Diagnosing from video has the full three-way read. Diagnose there before you change frequency here.

Why this rule exists. The failure is common and it has a shape. A lifter carrying fatigue she has not recovered from shows degraded technique. The coach reads that as a skill deficit and prescribes more frequency. That adds fatigue, which degrades technique further. The program looks responsive and is digging.

The tell is when in the session it happens. A skill problem is there on the first rep of the first set. A fatigue problem appears later and gets worse. Her first program and Effort and intensity lean on the same distinction.

Watch for

Assuming the compound lifts need frequent practice to be maintained. They are not high-skill movements in the way a coach reaching for more frequency imagines. Once a client can perform one, keeping it does not take constant repetition.
