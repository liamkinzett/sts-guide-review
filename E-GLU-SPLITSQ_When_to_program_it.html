Glutes · E-GLU-SPLITSQ#When to program it

When to use it

The problem you notice. Her lower back finishes her glute lifts, or her glutes lag despite plenty of glute work. Or one leg is behind the other. Or her squat shifts to one side under a bar. The split squat family answers each of those, and its two main versions do different jobs.

The split squat family has two main jobs. The Bulgarian builds her glutes and quads one leg at a time. The FFE (front-foot-elevated) split squat teaches her back leg to open the hip when her lower back has been doing that job. Take them in turn.

The Bulgarian split squat

Use it as the single-leg glute and quad builder on lower days, after the main two-legged lift. Normal setup, dumbbells.

Who it suits. Any client who can keep her big toe, knee and sternum in line on one leg. If she can't yet, she masters the basic version before you load or bias it.

What it gets her. Glutes and quads built through the stretched bottom of the rep (lengthened position), plus hip control that two-legged lifts don't train.

Compared

columns: Opposite-hand dumbbell | Goblet hold

Use it when: You want to bias her glutes. | You want to bias her quads.

Why it works: A dumbbell in the hand opposite the working leg (contralateral load) encourages a bigger hip shift. The hip shift is what loads the glute. | Held like a goblet squat, the weight sits in front of her centre of mass. The resistance sits closer to the knee, so the quads take more of it.

Also fixes: Her pelvis drifting to the back leg. | Nothing in particular. It is a loading choice.

Two don'ts. Don't stretch her stance out to "bias glutes". A long stance is a weaker position she can't load as well. And don't stack it straight after another hip-dominant lift that trains the same thing.

The FFE split squat

Use it when her lower back is doing her glutes' job. You will notice it in what she reports and on video. She feels hip thrusts, RDL lockouts or lunges in her lower back. Or her glutes lag despite plenty of glute work.

Confirm it with a bodyweight stationary lunge. Any of these confirms it:

  • She hinges forward instead of dropping straight down.
  • She shifts onto the front leg.
  • She arches her lower back.
  • She can't get her back knee behind her hip.

    • What it gets her. Her back leg learns to open the hip under load (hip extension). Then her glutes, not her lower back, finish the movement in every glute lift. Once she owns the position, progress her to the Bulgarian.

    Also for clients newer to single-leg work, because the FFE is a more stable base than the Bulgarian. And for clients stuck flat-footed.

    Setup. Front foot on a 1–2 inch plate. Torso upright. Back knee drives behind the hip.

    Cues

    When: Every rep of the FFE

    Say: Elevator, not escalator.

    Why: She drops straight down and comes straight up. A forward hinge turns it into a movement her lower back finishes.

    Loading. The same as the Bulgarian. Opposite-hand dumbbell to bias glutes, goblet hold to bias quads.

    Why the signs matter. Without full hip extension her body finds the range by arching her lower back (lumbar finish). Three things follow. Her lower back takes load meant for her glutes. Her glutes miss work at their squeezed end. And single-leg work stalls, because she escapes the position instead of holding it. See Faults and what you can actually see.

    Using a split squat to fix something

    The situations below are the corrective uses. They apply when a split squat is being used to fix a specific problem, not as the default reason to program one.

    Reach for it when

    # Her squat shows a visible hip or weight shift

    sub: And the restriction behind it is on one side

    Why it happens: Commonly an ankle on one side that won't bend forward (dorsiflexion). Under a two-legged bar she shifts away from it.

    Do this: A split squat. The split stance removes the thing producing the shift. She keeps training the torso and shin angles you actually wanted, without the shift.

    # She can't yet stabilise what she can load

    sub: You want to cap force output on purpose

    Why it happens: Her strength has outrun her control. On two legs she can produce more force than she can steer.

    Do this: A split squat. The single-leg setting takes a little off the top of her force production, and that is the point rather than the cost. She builds strength under control rather than on top of a wobble.

    # Her session is short

    sub: One lower-body slot has to do the work of several

    Do this: A split squat. It trains stability and strength together in one movement, and it is very loadable. This is not the soft option.

    # One leg is behind the other

    sub: A two-legged lift keeps letting the strong side cover

    Do this: A split squat. The weak side can't be carried, so the gap gets closed directly.

    Three things to hold onto

    It is an entry point, not a destination. Program it to solve something, and have the thing it leads back to. That is usually her squat and her deadlift.

    If you swap the exercise, keep the stimulus. Swapping a heavy squat for a split squat and then under-loading it is a common error. It removes the stimulus along with the risk. That is a coaching error, not an exercise one.

    Not in an early low-back rehab phase. See Pain rules and when to refer.

    Terms on this page

    lengthened position: The point in the rep where the working muscle is at its longest while it carries the load. It describes the muscle's length, not whether she feels a stretch.

    contralateral load: The weight is held in the hand opposite the working leg.

    hip extension: Opening the hip so the thigh travels behind the body line. It is the glute's main job, and true hip extension only happens when her knee gets behind her hip.

    lumbar finish: Finishing a hip movement by arching the lower back instead of extending the hip. Her back takes the load meant for her glutes, and she misses the glute's squeezed end.

    dorsiflexion: The ankle bending so the shin and the top of the foot move closer together, as when the toes pull up toward the shin or the shin travels forward over the foot. A stiff ankle on one side makes her shift away from it in a two-legged squat.
