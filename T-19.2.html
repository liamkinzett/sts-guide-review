How to read an exercise page · T-19.2

Every section answers one question

An exercise page has a fixed set of sections, in coaching order, and each one answers a single question. Knowing the question is what makes a page quick to read under time pressure. You go to the section that holds your question and read nothing else.

The sections, in the order they appear

# When to use it

Question: Which problem of hers does this movement solve, how do I confirm it, and what does it get her?

Also: The Library leads with the situation you would notice in her, not with the anatomy.

# What it's for

Question: Why is this movement in the program at all? Where is it hardest, which muscle length does it load (lengthened position) and which slot does it fill?

# Setup

Question: What must be true before rep one?

# How to do it

Question: What is the movement standard, top to bottom?

# Where it's hardest

Question: Where in the range does the tension peak, and why? The page says where. Tempo and pauses decides what she does there.

# Cues

Question: What do I say, and in what order? Two to four cues, first one first. Each is marked internal or external.

# Common faults

Question: What goes wrong, what does it look like on video, and what is the fix?

# Swaps

Question: What does she do instead when the equipment, a joint or the room says no?

# Choosing the equipment

Question: Barbell, dumbbell, machine or cable: what does each one change? Only on pages where the choice matters.

# Watch for

Question: Which injury cautions, populations and prerequisites apply, and which topic owns them?

# Variations

Question: What is each programmed version FOR, and when do I choose it over its nearest sibling? A bare list of names is never enough here.

# A named note

Question: One point this exercise needs settled that no other section holds. The click before a deadlift pull, or when to choose the seated calf raise. Only on pages that need it.

No section ever carries a coach's name. Cues are printed as cues. You will meet "as so-and-so says" attached to a cue in other places. Strip the name and keep the cue. The Library does not claim that a particular coach said a particular cue, and neither should you.

Watch for

A cue arriving with a name attached. Strip the attribution, keep the cue. The words are what she needs; the byline is not.

Terms on this page

lengthened position: The point in the rep where the working muscle is at its longest while it carries the load. It describes the muscle's length, not whether she feels a stretch.
