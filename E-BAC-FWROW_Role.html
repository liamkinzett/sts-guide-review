What it's for

The free-weight row is a horizontal pull performed from an unsupported hinge. The defining feature is not the pull. It is the hinge she holds still for the whole set (isometric). Every argument on these pages turns on that.

The versions, each declared

# Single arm dumbbell row, same side forward

sub: Same foot forward as the rowing arm (ipsilateral). Lat-biased

Why: Elbow tucked, rowing until the elbow is in line with the trunk, driving toward the hip. It builds a column of vertical force straight into the ground and isolates the lat fibres. No hard pinch of the blade at the finish.

# Single arm dumbbell row, opposite side forward

sub: Opposite foot forward (contralateral). Lat-biased at the shoulder, with a deliberate trunk-rotation demand

Why: Set up facing the bench in a wide stance, back hip open, front knee stacked over the toe, hinged over. The lat is trained as the dynamic strap wrapping around the rib cage.

# Barbell bent over row, lat-biased setup

sub: Palms down (pronated grip) or palms up (supinated grip)

Why: Elbows tucked close to the side, bar drawn to the lower abdomen. No hard pinch of the blade at the finish.

# Barbell bent over row, upper-back-biased setup

sub: Either grip

Why: Elbows wide, the blades travelling their full range, full retraction demanded at the finish.

# Trap bar bent over row

sub: The heavy loadable row

Why: Reached by elimination once the rest of the family has run out of load. Systemic loading rather than isolation. See The trap bar row and the supinated grip.

# Dumbbell bent over row

sub: Hinge-position training

Why: The upper-body load exists to hold the torso still in the hinge. It has a role but no setup, cue or fault list of its own, and no bias of its own. Coach it to this page's standards, and if you prescribe it for size, declare the bias as you would for the barbell row.

The barbell row is taught with no bias attached

Where the barbell row is taught in detail, it is taught as a spinal-resiliency and hinge-control movement. The coaching attention sits on the torso, the feet and the bar path. No shoulder-blade instruction comes with it. The two barbell setups above come from applying the general lat and upper-back rule.

A barbell row prescribed for back hypertrophy needs a bias declared by whoever prescribes it.

Where it is hardest. Free-weight rows load hardest where the lever is longest (moment arm) and the load sits furthest from the body. That is the mid-range and the stretched bottom, the mirror image of the cable and machine family. It is part of why the finish is coached differently here.

The slot it fills. A send-it slot in a session. The single arm dumbbell row sits alongside the seated cable row as the loadable exercise that follows a high-skill, low-load opener (see Building a session). It is separately a staple because it shores up the third plane. That is the side-to-side and twisting control at the spine that the deadlift and the barbell row do not touch.

Terms on this page

isometric: A hold. The muscle works against the load but the joint does not move.

ipsilateral: Same side. On a single-arm row it means the rowing arm and the forward foot are on the same side.

contralateral: Opposite side. On a single-arm row it means the forward foot is on the opposite side to the rowing arm.

pronated grip: Palms facing down or away from her, knuckles up. On a barbell row it is the overhand grip.

supinated grip: Palms facing up or toward her. On a barbell row it is the underhand grip.

moment arm: The perpendicular distance between the joint axis and the line of force.
