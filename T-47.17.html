What she will ask you · T-47.17

"Shouldn't I change it up?"

She does not need to change it up. Constant change is what prevents progress. Muscle confusion and variety in training are incredibly overrated.

The mechanism. Progress needs enough time in an exercise to develop the skill that lets it be pushed harder. Constant change prevents exactly that. You never get good enough at anything to load it.

Change is a tool with one trigger and one rule. The trigger is a genuine plateau: no progress in weeks, with everything else she needs to be doing in order. Then maybe change the exercise, not for muscle confusion, but to add novelty. The rule is a like-for-like swap: similar pattern, similar stimulus. That is the answer to "so I should never change anything?". Progression owns plateaus as programming.

An analogy she can retell. A jet flies past and the sound arrives later. The adaptation is the sound wave. Visible progress is the first sign she is about to adapt, and the gap between the jet and the sound gets longer for some tissues. People see the jet go by and change the program. Wait for all the glass to shatter first.

Why switching early loses the adaptation

Skill builds | Weeks in one exercise, getting better at it

Load rises | Because she can now push it

Progress shows | The leading edge of an adaptation still arriving

Switch now? | Abandons it mid-arrival

A cautionary tale. A coach who changed his program weekly to shock the muscle spent that era wondering why his elbows hurt.

Say it like this

Getting bored isn't a sign it's stopped working. Getting good at a lift is what lets us load it, and that's where the results come from. If we genuinely stall for weeks, we'll swap like for like.

Where novelty does belong. Motivation and adherence owns engineering novelty inside the plan before she goes looking for it outside. A harder variation of the same pattern, then a return.

Watch for

Swapping the exercise the week it starts working. That is the jet going by.
