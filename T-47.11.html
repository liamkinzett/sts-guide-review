What she will ask you · T-47.11

"Why won't my calves grow?"

It is a dosing answer before it is a genetics answer. The first answer is arithmetic.

Two sets at the end of the session. Her calves will not grow because she does two sets at the end of her session. If you want to prioritise something, put it on the front end so she can get good at it.

The prescription that says the same thing. Where calves are a priority, train them first, two to three times a week. End-of-leg-day work is what halts growth. Calves and adductors owns the exercises.

Selection turns on knee angle, not fibre type. The calf has two muscles. The soleus is the deeper, flatter one, and it does most of the work of pointing the foot down when the knee is bent. The gastrocnemius is the bigger one you can see, and it works best with the knee straight. A seated calf raise is more soleus. A standing calf raise brings in the gastrocnemius.

Compared

columns: Seated calf raise | Standing calf raise

Knee: Bent | Straight

Muscle: More soleus | More gastrocnemius

Why forearms grow and calves do not. Forearms are loaded involuntarily by every hold, every curl and every hinge. Calves are not. That asymmetry, plus two sets at the end of a session, is most of the difference she is describing.

Genetics is the second answer, and it is an anecdote. A famous bodybuilder asked for calf tips was told: don't ask me, they just grow. Genetics is real. It is not where you start.

Say it like this

Before we blame your genetics, let's look at the dose. Two sets at the end of leg day isn't a calf program. We'll put them first, two or three times a week, and see what they do with a real dose.

What you cannot give her. Nothing here explains calves through fibre type, daily loading or tendon architecture. You hold a dosing answer and an anecdote.

Watch for

"Calves are genetic." Sometimes. Fix the dose first and find out.
