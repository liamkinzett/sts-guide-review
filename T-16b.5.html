Mobility and stretching · T-16b.5

Give her the routine, for adherence, not mobility

She asks for a stretching routine. Write her one. The reason is that she wants it and it is harmless once screened, never that it will improve her mobility.

Screen it first, then write it

Is there a diagnosis on her spine? Known thinning of the spinal bone (vertebral osteoporosis) or a fixed forward rounding of the upper back (kyphosis) changes the routine. Bending the spine forward under load, and fast twisting, are out. That removes sit-ups and several common yoga and Pilates positions. A client with that diagnosis is exactly the client most likely to ask for this routine.

Then say why you are writing it, in your own head first. You are writing it because she wants it. Because it is harmless for her once screened. Because telling a client to stop something she enjoys costs goodwill for nothing. You are not writing it to improve her mobility. Training through full range has already answered that. You are not writing it to keep her healthy either. Nothing shows stretching does that.

Why the reason matters. This topic spends four pages establishing that stretching does very little. It cannot then prescribe stretching on the strength of what it does, or it argues with itself. The justification has to sit outside what stretching achieves. A coach who later defends the routine on flexibility grounds has misread this page. That is the specific error it exists to prevent.

It goes in her session notes, not in the program. Write it out for her where she will read it. It does not go in as exercise entries with sets, reps and a load to log against. The moment it looks like programmed work it starts competing with programmed work. That is the whole of "stretching is not training" expressed in where you type it.

Be straight with her on timing too. The routine goes after training or on its own day. The holds a client actually wants are the long ones. Short holds under sixty seconds before a session are prep work, not her routine.

When you may deviate. If the routine starts displacing training, eating session time or becoming the thing she believes is driving her results, it has stopped being harmless. The straight conversation is now overdue.

Say it like this

The thing that is actually changing your range is what you are already doing. Squatting to depth, pulling from the floor, full range under load, three times a week. That is your mobility work. Happy to write you a stretching routine on top of that if you enjoy it. I will put it in your notes rather than your program. It is not what is driving the result and I do not want it competing with your sessions.

Lead with what is working before you name what does not. The three facts are the same either way. Opening with the routine's limits reads as a brush-off to a client who asked a reasonable question. It also buries the genuinely good news, which is that her training is already doing the job.

Terms on this page

vertebral osteoporosis: The bones of the spine have lost density and can fracture under loads a healthy spine would tolerate.

kyphosis: An exaggerated forward rounding of the upper back. It is usually diagnosed by a clinician.
