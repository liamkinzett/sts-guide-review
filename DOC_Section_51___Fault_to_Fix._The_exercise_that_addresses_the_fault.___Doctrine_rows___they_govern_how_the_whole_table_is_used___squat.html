Three rules behind every fix, and the squat's other rows

Three rules govern how you use every row in this topic. If cueing is not working, the exercise is wrong. A regression moves along two axes only. And the constraint you apply is also your assessment.

The three rules

# If cueing is not working, the exercise is wrong

sub: Constrain it or regress it

What it means: You may have given her an exercise she is not ready for yet. Regress the exercise and improve from there. This is the hinge between the cue pages and this topic: the cue failing is what sends you here.

# A regression has two axes, and only two

sub: Move the centre of mass, or change the base of support

What it means: Bring the load in front of her, or take it away. Widen the base, or narrow it: two dowels to one dowel to none, two legs to one leg. That is what makes a regression sustainable and progressable. Unstable surfaces are not on the ladder.

# The constraint is also the assessment

sub: Apply the fix and watch whether the fault survives it

What it means: Add a heel wedge or an anterior load and see what happens. If she hinges her squat anyway, that has told you a lot. Diagnosing from video calls this constraint as test.

A constraint with no exit plan is not a fix

The long-term goal is to make up the deficit, not to rely on the wedge. A heel lift she never comes off is a crutch. Schedule its removal when you add it.

Lateral hip shift. Her hips move sideways out of the hole, and she loads one leg more than the other. Region by region owns the judgement of when a shift matters, because a shift can be how she produces force. Ask that first. Once you have decided it does matter, this is what you do.

Lateral hip shift on the squat

# Neutralise the pelvis before you load the bar

sub: Accessory, and the order is the prescription

What you see: She shifts to one side as she stands up.

Do this: Put her on a lying hamstring curl and cue both hip bones (ASIS) to press into the pad equally. That neutralises the pelvis. Do it before you go loading the bar, not alongside it. A coach who adds the curl and leaves the squat weight where it was has not followed this.

Exercises: Lying hamstring curl

# Unilateral work instead of loading the pattern she shifts out of

sub: Regression

Do this: Single-leg squatting or hinging: a split squat, a lunge, a step-down. Not more weight on the two-legged pattern she shifts out of.

Exercises: DB Bulgarian split squat; DB reverse lunge; Step-down

Setup points that belong to no single fault. Four setup points a coach needs that fit under no one fault name. Two are for the squat and two for the deadlift.

Setup points with no fault name

# A cotton T-shirt for a lifter new to low bar

sub: Not a singlet, not bare skin

Why: The bar slips on a singlet, and on bare skin the first time it is a lot more abrasive. Have her bring a T-shirt.

# Toes out about 20 degrees, feet about shoulder width

sub: A moderate angle with a moderate stance

Why: That pairing is the measured moderate setup. In low bar, narrow stances measure around 20 degrees of toe-out, medium around 23 and wide around 26. Start moderate and adjust from what her squat shows you.

# Stance and hips take priority over grip width

sub: Written for the deadlift, and it reaches most STS clients

Why: Forcing an arms-straight-down grip on a lifter with narrower shoulders than hips pushes the bar forward and the weight onto the toes. Set the stance first and the grip around it. Diagnosing from video carries the same rule.

# Take the slack out of the bar before every pull

sub: The wedge, and it is on the deadlift pages

Why: Pull the slack out of the bar with the shoulders and lats and push into the ground at the same time. Arms long until you hear an audible click. The conventional deadlift page and What to say when I see a fault carry the wording.

Squat faults where the fix is a cue, or lives on another page. These faults have a name and no exercise change of their own. Cue them from What to say when I see a fault and work the exercise page. Do not invent an accessory for them.

Squat faults with a cue and no exercise change

# Lumbar finish

sub: She finishes the rep by arching her lower back (lumbar finish)

Do this: There is no exercise change for it on the squat. A belt does not prevent it, so do not reach for one as the fix. Watch the finish and cue it.

# Bar off the midline

sub: The bar drifts forward off the line

Do this: The setup fix is the same as the deadlift's, and it has its own page in this topic. Reset her midfoot position, then re-tighten the lat brace.

# Butt wink

sub: Her back rounds at depth as the pelvis tucks under

Do this: On a back-supported machine, limit depth to her active range. On a free squat, find what her depth responds to. What to do about a butt wink, in Quads, is the procedure.

# Knees driven out too hard

sub: She squats on the outside edges of her feet

Do this: Stop the "knees out" cue. On a split squat, a weight held in the opposite hand (contralateral load) steers the knee back over the foot. There is no two-legged accessory for it.

# Ankle dumping in

sub: The arch collapses and she rolls onto the inside of the foot

Do this: The two fixes are split-squat fixes. Its own page in this topic carries them, and says why they do not automatically transfer to a two-footed squat.

# Weight into the heels

sub: The toes lift as she stands

Do this: Cue "full foot pressure". Take her shoes off for the sensory feedback. There is no accessory and no regression for it.

# Head as a release valve

sub: The head tips or flares to release tension

Do this: A cue, and it is on the bench and deadlift pages. Nothing squat-specific.

# Rib flare

sub: Ribs splayed, lower back arched

Do this: Cue "ribs to hips". The Copenhagen hold is an indirect accessory, and its own page in this topic says how far to trust it.

Three of those are foot-and-knee faults, and every fix for them was written for the split squat. A two-footed variation hides what a single-leg one exposes, which is why the single-leg version is where they get fixed.

Terms on this page

ASIS: The two bony points you can feel at the front of the pelvis, one each side, just below the waistband. Pressing both into a pad equally squares the pelvis.

contralateral load: The weight is held in the hand opposite the working leg.

lumbar finish: Finishing a hip movement by arching the lower back instead of extending the hip.
