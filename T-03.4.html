Fatigue and recovery · T-03.4

What do coaches mean by central and peripheral fatigue?

When she says her nervous system is fried (CNS), do not treat that as a diagnosis. Translate it into questions you can act on:

  • is her performance actually down;
  • is it local or widespread;
  • is the issue sleep, soreness, stress or training dose;
  • and has it happened once, or is it repeating?

    • Physiologists split this kind of tiredness into two. One is tiredness that starts upstream of the muscle, in the nervous system itself (central fatigue). The other is tiredness that sits at, or inside, the muscle (peripheral fatigue). The two can be present together. You rarely need to prove which share of her problem is which. What the split does is stop you treating every "fried" complaint as if it only belongs to the muscle.

    On the floor, that usually shows up as one of two things. Coaches rarely say "central" or "peripheral" out loud. They say "neurological" for the nervous-system kind and "metabolic" for the muscle kind. Those are loose coaching labels for the same split, not two more categories on top of it. Ask which she means.

    Compared

    columns: Neurological (central) | Metabolic (peripheral)

    What she means: Her nerves are not telling the muscles to do the right thing. She is not cognitively clear | The muscles themselves are not working at their peak

    What she says: "I can't focus", "I feel flat", "the bar felt wrong" | "My legs had nothing", "everything was heavy"

    Why it matters when you prescribe. They do not resolve on the same timescale and they do not respond to the same change. A blanket deload answers one of them by accident. Find out which one she has, then change the thing that fixes it.

    How you find out. There is no gym test that separates them. The split is a way of thinking, not a protocol. You get the answer by asking her and listening to how she describes it.

    Terms on this page

    cns: The central nervous system, the brain and spinal cord. Coaches use the phrase for the nerve side of fatigue, when the signal to the muscle is what has dropped.

    central fatigue: Tiredness in the nervous system and the whole body, not in one muscle.

    peripheral fatigue: Tiredness that sits in the muscle itself, or at the point where the nerve meets it, rather than in the central nervous system. It fades as the muscle adapts to the work.
