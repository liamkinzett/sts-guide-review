Tempo and pauses · T-15.17

Two places tempo never changes

The working tempo holds through an intensifier, and it holds through a deload. Both are the same answer: tempo is a control, not a lever.

Through an intensifier, the working tempo holds. An intensifier is part of the set it hangs off. That means a drop set (strip weight and keep going) or a rest-pause (a short rest, then more reps). It also means a myorep extension (mini-sets after the hard set). Rest and intensity techniques covers them. Each runs at that exercise's tempo.

This matters because at failure a three second lowering half (eccentric) is genuinely hard to hold. The reps most likely to be rushed are the ones the technique was bought for. If she cannot hold the tempo through the drop, the drop is too heavy.

In a deload, tempo does not change. The phases answers deload fatigue with volume alone. The exercises, the load and the effort all stay where they were. The eccentric she is holding is the same one she held last week, on fewer sets. Changing tempo on top would move a second variable in the one week designed to move only one. It would also cost the week's numbers, because tempo is part of the context you hold still.

Why it works. The instinct to reach for tempo when a session needs to be made easier is the instinct to break the measurement. Fatigue gets handled by load, by selection or by volume. All three are levers. Tempo is not.

Watch for

The deload written at a slower tempo because slower feels lighter. It is not lighter. It is a different exercise with the same name.

Terms on this page

deload: A planned easier week that lets fatigue clear while the muscle and the skill stay.

tempo: The four-digit code that sets how many seconds each phase of a rep takes: lowering, pause, lifting, pause.

eccentric: The lowering half of a rep, where the muscle lengthens under load.
