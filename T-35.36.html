Pain rules and when to refer · T-35.36

A knock to the head stops the session

Verdict

Any knock to the head ends her session. Any one of nine critical signs sends her to an emergency department today.

You never clear her yourself. If in doubt, sit her out.

A dropped bar, a fall from a box, a rack collision and a bar to the back of the head are all this page. None of them needs to look dramatic. The standing instruction is simple: if in doubt, sit them out.

Red flags

title: The nine critical signs. Any one of them and she goes to the nearest emergency department today

Neck pain

Increasing confusion, agitation or irritability

Repeated vomiting

Seizure or convulsion

Weakness or tingling/burning in the arms or legs

Deteriorating conscious state

Severe or increasing headache

Unusual behavioural change

Double vision

emergency: A critical sign may mean a more serious injury. She is taken immediately to the nearest emergency department. She does not drive herself.

Three columns, not one list. The guidance sorts signs into critical, obvious and subtle. Only the critical column is an emergency. The obvious column includes loss of consciousness, confusion or disorientation, memory impairment, and being unsteady on her feet or having balance problems. Any of those means she is done for the day and needs a medical assessment before she trains again. Running the columns together would promote every headache to an ambulance and leave the critical list meaning nothing.

Compared

columns: Critical sign | Obvious sign

What it means: A more serious injury may be present | A head knock with symptoms, not an emergency

Do this: Nearest emergency department, immediately. She does not drive | Done for the day. Medical assessment before she trains again

Examples: Any of the nine above | Loss of consciousness, confusion or disorientation, memory impairment, unsteady on her feet or balance problems

You do not clear her. Not the same day, not the next session, not because she says she is fine. Clearance belongs to a medical practitioner, and the session she comes back to is theirs to authorise, not yours. The return-to-sport stages, the stand-down periods and the recognition tool all belong to that practitioner too. You never run a recognition tool as an assessment. What you need is three things: stop, refer, and do not clear.

Say it like this

That's you done today. I need you seen before you lift again.

Do not negotiate it, and do not let her drive herself anywhere if any critical sign is present.
