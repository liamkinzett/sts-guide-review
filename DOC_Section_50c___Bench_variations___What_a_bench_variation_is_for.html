What a bench variation is for

The competition bench is the strength standard. Every variation changes one thing about it: the range, the grip, the speed, or the support. A variation is chosen for a job. There are five.

The five jobs a bench variation does

# Fixing a specific fault

sub: The variation removes the fault by its shape

What it looks like: The Spoto press for a lifter whose position degrades on the way down. A three-count pause bench for a lifter who cannot hold tension on the chest. A low-incline press as a constraint for the client whose sternum is stuck down.

# The same movement at a lower technical demand

sub: The press slot that refines technique after the heavy work

What it looks like: Close grip, chains, boards and the floor press. The same motor pattern, less technical demand.

# Overloading where the lockout needs it

sub: For the lifter whose triceps run out

What it looks like: A board or block press for a long-limbed lifter, or one benching in a shirt.

# A second exposure to the pattern

sub: The strength system's second pressing day

What it looks like: Competition bench on day one, then a close grip or an incline as the second exposure (exposure). For a bigger lifter, an overhead press and a chest press machine.

# Building tissue early in a peak

sub: Variations carry the first weeks

What it looks like: Tempo bench in weeks 1 to 4 of the block plate. Board press, floor press, then close grip as the max-effort rotation in the conjugate plate.

Every barbell press variation but one loads the stretched and mid positions and gives nothing at the squeezed end. Presses work the chest where it is long (lengthened position) and through the middle of the range. They give nothing where the chest is bunched short (shortened position). The block press is the exception. It removes the bottom and leaves mid-to-shortened, so it is the one barbell press that loads the shortened end.

Terms on this page

exposure: One session in the week where a lift, a pattern or a muscle is trained.

lengthened position: The point in the rep where the working muscle is at its longest while it carries the load. It describes the muscle's length, not whether she feels a stretch.

shortened position: The muscle is working while it is bunched short. On a press that is the top, arms locked out.

block plate: One of the three 12-week peaking programs in Peaking for a meet, built as three four-week blocks.

conjugate plate: The 12-week peaking program in Peaking for a meet that rotates a heavy max-effort variation every one to two weeks. It needs bands, chains and boards.
