When to use it

The problem you notice. Her deadlift is heavy and her spine only ever meets load standing tall. Nothing in her week asks her trunk to hold a hinge while the load moves. Or her deadlift and barbell row are covered and nothing trains her trunk against twisting. The free-weight row is the movement whose defining feature is the hinge she holds, not the pull.

A hinge is bending forward from the hips with the back held flat and still, the way she sets up for a deadlift. On this row she holds that shape while the arms work.

Reach for it when

# Her spine is the thing you are training and the row is how you load it

sub: Her trunk has never held a hinge under a moving load

Confirm it: Look at her week. Every hinge she does is a deadlift, where the load moves with the spine. Nothing asks her to hold the hinge still while the arms work.

What it gets her: Reactive control in the hinge under a fraction of a deadlift's load. Hinged over, gravity acts across the spine rather than down it. The deep stabilisers have to switch on by themselves, because she cannot contract them on purpose. This is STS's row for building spinal resiliency.

# Her deadlift and barbell row are covered and nothing touches the third plane

sub: Both of those lifts are forward-and-back only

Confirm it: List her hinges. All of them are two-handed and symmetrical.

What it gets her: Control at the spine against side-to-side and twisting forces (frontal plane) (transverse plane). The single arm dumbbell row is a staple because it shores up the third plane those two lifts do not reach.

# She is hesitant about technique and you want the hinge taught, not tested

sub: She is not ready to be tested under a bar

Confirm it: She has a hinge you would not load heavily yet, and she knows it.

What it gets her: The same high-shear position under far less load. The barbell row is deliberately less loadable out of the gate, and that is a feature.

# The family has run out of load and you want a heavy row

sub: Systemic loading rather than isolation

Confirm it: She has progressed through the single arm dumbbell row and the pull-up and there is no heavier row left.

What it gets her: A lot of load on her skeleton. The trap bar row is reached by elimination: a heavier loadable row, for power, not isolation. Never select it as a hypertrophy row.

The low back failing first is designed in

It is not a coaching failure, but it decides the selection. If back hypertrophy is the goal and the spinal muscles end the set, the answer is the chest-supported family. That is a goal-driven swap, not a downgrade.

Name the goal, because the goal decides the row. The barbell row is kept for spinal resiliency. Chest-supported rowing replaces it when the hinge ends the set before the back reaches failure. Neither is superior. After machine rows she feels the target muscle. After barbell rows the core and the low back are taxed too. Those are two different jobs, and you say which one the row is doing before you write it down.

The landmine row. STS doesn't program it. It appears only as the last rung of a stability ladder, with no bias declared, and nothing on this page should be read onto it.

Setup. Chest over the knees, whole foot on the floor, ribs down. The cues and faults carry the standards.

Terms on this page

frontal plane: Side-to-side movement, like a lateral lunge or a side raise.

transverse plane: Twisting movement around the long axis of the body, like a chop, a throw or a rotational lunge. A single-arm row asks the trunk to resist it.
