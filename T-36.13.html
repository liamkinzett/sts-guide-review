A hip shift may be information rather than a fault

When she shifts sideways over one leg, ask first whether that shift is how she produces force. It may be the answer rather than the fault.

Why. Producing force in mid-stance needs the thigh to turn inward in the hip socket (internal rotation). With the foot fixed, that rotation is made by the pelvis travelling sideways (frontal plane) over a femur that cannot move. The lateral shift IS the internal rotation.

Blocking it creates the problem it is blamed for. A coach stops the pelvis translating on a step-up or a split squat, sometimes by putting the step against a wall. She then sees a weak glute and names it. She made closed-chain internal rotation impossible, so force production impossible, then blamed the client.

The constraint that makes the shift useful: keep the foot fixed. Ten toes down, even pressure. Otherwise the femur rocks with the pelvis and no relative rotation happens.

This is a judgement call. A shift can be a fault to correct, and Faults and what you can actually see treats it as one on a two-legged squat. It can also be information: the side she shifts toward has more rotation available. A coach who corrects a shift is not wrong, provided she has asked first whether the shift is how this client is producing force.

Watch for

"Knees out" as a blanket cue. It turns the thigh outward (external rotation). Hip internal rotation is usually the actual limiter.

Terms on this page

frontal plane: Side-to-side movement, like a lateral lunge or a side raise.

internal rotation: Turning a limb inward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face back or down, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more toward the middle. The muscles that do it are the internal rotators.

external rotation: Turning a limb outward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face forward or up, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more to the side. A muscle that does it is an external rotator.
