Plane complexity decides where you intervene

Movement happens in three planes. Forwards and back (sagittal plane), side to side (frontal plane), and rotation (transverse plane). They increase in complexity in that order. Reverse the order to regress.

A rotation limitation is the most downstream problem there is. It is the last place to intervene. Rarely, if ever, does a limitation exist in the rotational plane without a deficiency in the forwards-and-back plane underneath it.

So when she presents lacking rotation, peel back. Assess the simpler planes first. What looks like a rotation problem is usually far easier to see, and to fix, as a forwards-and-back one.

Why it works. The same idea is a progression dial. To make a movement harder, move it a plane outward: a split squat becomes a lateral lunge, then a rotational lunge. To make it easier, move it inward.

This is the usual choice, not an absolute. One exception sits at the shoulder: some high-level throwing athletes genuinely have an isolated rotational limitation. You are unlikely to coach one. If you do, do not assume the simpler plane is at fault.

Terms on this page

sagittal plane: Forward-and-back movement, like a squat, a hinge or a curl.

frontal plane: Side-to-side movement, like a lateral lunge or a side raise.

transverse plane: Twisting movement around the long axis of the body, like a chop, a throw or a rotational lunge. The most complex plane, and a limitation here almost always sits on top of one in a simpler plane.
