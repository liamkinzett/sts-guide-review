What to do when a shoulder clicks on an overhead press

A click is not one thing. It splits three ways, and the split decides everything. Ask her which it is before you touch the program.

Work through it in this order

Test. Pain, discomfort, or only a noise? Then the overhead reach, unloaded.

Read. Run the three buckets. For a painless click, the sorted variable is the noise.

Do. Train what does not provoke it. Name your working read, as a guess.

Change. Press angle, grip and range are the dials before the movement comes out.

Escalate. Pain arriving, swelling, locking, the joint giving way, a limb going numb or weak. None of it waits.

Step 1. Test

Which of three is it?

? Is it pain, in the sense of a warning?

yes: Stop. Pain plus clicking is a referral. Refer and change the plan.

? Is it discomfort, the set working?

yes: Sort it by the sensation, exactly as the bucket method has it.

= Only a noise, nothing felt. This page. The sorted variable becomes the click.

Do not accept

A client with no practice at interpreting sensation reports everything that does not feel good as pain. The fix is the question form: not "how did that feel" but "what did you feel". The second makes her name it.

Then the overhead reach, unloaded, read in order. Can she hold rib cage over pelvis? If no, that is the whole intervention and you stop there. Then look for the shoulder blade rotating upward while staying down.

Step 2. Read

Run the three buckets with one substitution. The bucket method sorts by whether a movement makes it feel better or worse. A painless click has no sensation to sort. So the sorted variable becomes the noise: does this movement make the click more, less, or no different?

Step 3. Do

Train the palliative and no-effect buckets. That is the whole session. Rank the provocative bucket from most tolerable to least. That ranking is the order things come back.

Your working read. Name it, label it a guess

Scope first. This is for aches in people who are training now: "my shoulder aches when I go overhead". It is not for someone two weeks after surgery on a torn labrum or a cuff repair. And the first question is whether it is an injury at all, or pain, or discomfort. She does not have to have a torn labrum for the front of her shoulder to hurt.

What you see, what you might guess, what changes on Monday

# Pain on top of the shoulder, worst at the end of a press, grip wide

sub: Working read: the AC joint

Why it happens: Going too wide loads the joint on top of the shoulder (AC joint) rather than the delt. Structure is taking what function should.

Do this: Set the grip to her own shoulder width. Never load the hands inside the AC joint plane either: no plate press, diamond push-up, very narrow bench or crossover rear delt fly. If she wants the arms-in path, hands drawn in toward the midline (adducted), row single arm.

# AC pain that does not resolve with grip width

sub: Look along the chain, not at the joint

Why it happens: The other end of the collarbone is the joint at the breastbone (SC joint). If that joint cannot move, the collarbone cannot move, so the AC joint cannot do AC joint things.

Do this: Progression, not avoidance. Front and back ribs move, then the SC joint, then the shoulder blade, then the AC joint gains motion. Start in lower ranges of reaching forward and out, and progress higher.

# Pain at the front of the shoulder pressing overhead, elbows flared

sub: Working read: compression under the roof of the shoulder (subacromial compression)

Why it happens: Flaring the elbows lifts the upper arm out to the side, which jams it against the bony roof and squashes the tissue underneath.

Do this: Tuck the elbows for a pain-free path. A high pulley turns the upper arm outward (external rotation) and tilts the plane upward, with much less turning force on the joint (torque).

# Pain along the front of the upper arm with a wide grip

sub: Working read: the long head of the biceps

Why it happens: Wide is more arm-out-to-the-side (abduction) and less rotation. It calls on the secondary flexors and extensors, not the cuff, because there is not enough rotational torque to call on it.

Do this: Narrow toward her own shoulder width and restore some rotation to the path.

# Shoulder blades pushing forward or riding up at the top under a heavy bar

sub: A severe technique fault, not a tissue problem

Why it happens: It jams the joint and makes a rotator cuff injury highly likely.

Do this: Fix the shoulder blade position before load.

# Diffuse, poorly located, no clear provoking position

sub: A less strong position, not a diagnosis

Why it happens: Clinicians will offer buzzwords. None of them changes what you do.

Do this: The bucket sort, and build the position.

The order of attack, and it is not negotiable inside this region. Work from the inside out: upper back, then shoulder blade, then rotator cuff, then the muscles that turn the arm inward (internal rotators). Do not start at the cuff.

Two corrections to standard practice

The rotator cuff's job is not to rotate her with a dumbbell overhead. Its job is to keep the joint in place. It is not a prime mover, and progressive overload is the wrong frame for cuff work. It is trained by taking the joint away from its centre, not by adding load to a band.

Before you say "weak rotator cuff", ask which muscle, and whether "weak" is even the right axis.

A working read never changes how it is said

It is a best guess, named with the proper clinical term, with a physio to confirm it. Never graded. Never a reason to delay a referral. Holding a read is not the same as stating a diagnosis.

How a working read is said

My best guess from what I'm seeing, and it is a guess, not a diagnosis, is the AC joint at the top of your shoulder. A physio confirms that. Meanwhile we bring your grip in to shoulder width and see what the click does.

Step 4. Change

Press angle, grip and range are the dials before the movement comes out. The overhead press page in Shoulders runs the same ladder. Keep everything the sort did not flag.

Step 5. Escalate

Red flags

title: None of these waits

Pain arriving on a click that was painless

Swelling, locking, or the joint giving way

A limb going numb or weak

Night pain, or a loss of range, on the general list

emergency: A limb going numb or weak is referred immediately and is never sorted first.

We give no sets or reps for a rehabilitating shoulder. Her physio sets the dose. You set the position, and you keep the rest of her training going.

Terms on this page

AC joint: The acromioclavicular joint, where the collarbone meets the top of the shoulder blade. You can feel it as the bony bump on top of the shoulder.

SC joint: The sternoclavicular joint, where the inner end of the collarbone meets the breastbone. If it cannot move, nothing further along the collarbone can either.

subacromial compression: Tissue squashed between the top of the upper arm bone and the bony roof of the shoulder. It happens when the elbow flares out as she presses.

adducted: The upper arm held in close to the side of the body. An adducted row finishes with the hands close in.

external rotation: Turning a limb outward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face forward or up, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more to the side. A muscle that does it is an external rotator.

torque: The turning force on a joint. The further the load sits from the joint, the more of it there is.

abduction: A limb travelling out and away from the midline of the body: the arm in a lateral raise, or the thigh moving out to the side.

internal rotators: The muscles that turn the upper arm inward in the socket, mainly the chest and the lats.
