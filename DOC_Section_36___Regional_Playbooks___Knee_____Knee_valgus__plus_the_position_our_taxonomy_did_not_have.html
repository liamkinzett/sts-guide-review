Knee valgus: when knees-in is a fault and when it is not

Whether her knees coming in is a fault depends on depth. Knees coming in is (knee valgus). Above 90 degrees it is a fault. Below parallel it is not.

The argument, in three steps.

1. The squat is a rotational movement. Descending loads the thigh turning outward (external rotation) and moving out to the side (abduction). Standing up requires the thigh to turn back inward (internal rotation) to bring the knee back into line so the quad can produce force. Deeper means more abduction. More internal rotation is then needed to stand.

2. Depth decides whether knees-in is a fault at all.

PresentationVerdict
Knees caving above 90 degrees, not squatting to depthA genuine fault. The knees were never out enough to justify any force inward
Knees travelling in below parallel, hip crease under the kneeNot a fault. Past 90 degrees of hip bend, the deep buttock muscle (piriformis) switches from an external rotator to an internal rotator. The knees coming in is loading the hip stabilisers

3. Women have a wider angle from hip to knee. That angle is the (Q angle). The thigh bone leaves the pelvis on an inward path toward the midline. A woman's neutral is therefore a more knees-in position than a man's. Her soft tissue is laid down for it, because that is how she walks around all day. Critics of women's knee travel are applying a male alignment standard to a different skeleton.

What this does not change

You still cannot screen knee valgus by eye. Faults and what you can actually see classes it as named but not screenable. That is about what a coach can see, not about what is happening.

Terms on this page

knee valgus: Her knees fall inwards, inside the line of her toes, as she squats or stands up.

piriformis: A deep muscle in the buttock that rotates the hip. Past 90 degrees of hip bend its line of pull changes and it rotates the hip inward instead of outward.

Q angle: The angle between the line of the thigh and the line of the shin at the knee. A wider pelvis gives a larger angle, so the knee sits further inside the hip.

external rotation: Turning a limb outward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face forward or up, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more to the side. A muscle that does it is an external rotator.

abduction: A limb travelling out and away from the midline of the body: the arm in a lateral raise, or the thigh moving out to the side.

internal rotation: Turning a limb inward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face back or down, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more toward the middle. The muscles that do it are the internal rotators.
