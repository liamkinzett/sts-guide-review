Why a smaller lifter can out-lift a bigger one

A smaller lifter can out-lift a bigger one because strength is partly a skill of the nervous system, not only a matter of muscle size.

The previous page showed this inside one client. The same mechanism answers the question between two clients.

The mechanism. The size of the muscle (cross-sectional area) sets a ceiling on force. It does not set the whole outcome. Strength expression is governed separately, by the nervous system, coordination and skill with the load.

Between two lifters. A smaller lifter who is further along the nervous-system and skill side can express more strength from less muscle. A larger lifter may carry more tissue but not yet have the same efficiency at using it. Tissues adapt in a fixed order, nervous system before muscle. The fast-adapting side can separate from the tissue that is still catching up, in either direction.

What this does not do. Do not reach for limb length, leverage or where her tendons attach (anthropometrics) as the explanation. The nervous-system and skill difference is the mechanism we teach, and it answers the question on its own.

Say it like this

Strength isn't just muscle size. It's also how efficiently your nervous system uses the muscle you've got. Someone smaller who has trained that skill for longer can out-lift someone bigger who hasn't, on the same amount of tissue or less.

Watch for

Treating this as evidence that hypertrophy training does not matter. Both are real, separately trainable outcomes. This page explains a divergence. It does not rank the two adaptations against each other.

Terms on this page

cross-sectional area: How thick a muscle is when cut straight across. It is the usual measure of muscle size and it sets the ceiling on how much force the muscle can produce.

anthropometrics: Her body's proportions and build. Limb lengths, torso length and where tendons attach to bone.
