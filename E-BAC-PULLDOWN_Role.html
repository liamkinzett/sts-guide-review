What it's for

The lat pulldown is STS's seated vertical pull. Neutral and palms-facing grips are the lat-biased setup. The palms-away grip is the upper-back option. Pick the grip from the goal.

First the words. Palms facing each other or facing in is a neutral grip (neutral grip). Palms facing her is a supinated grip (supinated grip). Palms facing away from her is a pronated grip (pronated grip).

Compared

columns: Neutral or supinated | Pronated

Target: The lats. | The upper back.

Why: Both grips let her reach the true overhead position with the upper arm turned outward (external rotation). That is where the lat is longest. | It widens the arm path. A wider path moves the work off the lats and onto the upper back.

How we coach it: Shoulder blades down and locked. Elbow tucked toward the torso, driving toward the hip crease. | A wider arm path with the upper back doing the work. It is a different exercise for a different target.

The pronated pulldown is a great upper-back exercise. It is not inferior and it is not a fallback for when no other handle exists. It is just different. A wider arm path makes it harder to reach the true overhead position, so the lat is loaded less and the upper back more. That shift is the whole point of the grip when the upper back is the target. It is not a flaw and it is not a joint risk.

When to choose it. When the upper back is the goal. Do not choose it as the lat-biased grip; for the lat, the other two grips reach the overhead position better.

What the movement demands. The main demand is shoulder extension, the arm travelling down and back. Second is the arm coming in toward the body (adduction). The movement is mostly forward-and-back (sagittal plane). Getting overhead demands three things. The arm travelling forward and up (shoulder flexion). The upper arm turning outward (external rotation). The shoulder blade turning upward (upward rotation). That is why the setup is so position-sensitive.

Resistance is continuous. On a cable column tension never drops. The productive end is the stretched overhead position (lengthened), which is exactly what an over-wide grip destroys.

The slot it fills. The first lat exercise a client is given, in single-arm form. The diagnostic when a back is not growing. The scaled entry point for a shoulder that will not tolerate overhead loading. And the pull-before-press step in an overhead ladder, since a lifter organises a position more easily when she is pulled there first.

Terms on this page

neutral grip: Palms facing each other, or facing in toward her body. On a pulldown it keeps the shoulder externally rotated on the way overhead.

supinated grip: Palms facing up or toward her. On a pulldown it preserves the overhead position the same way a neutral grip does.

pronated grip: Palms facing down or away from her, knuckles up. On a pulldown it widens the arm path and moves the work onto the upper back.

adduction: A limb travelling in toward the midline of the body: the leg pulled in by the inner-thigh muscles, or the arm brought in to the side.

sagittal plane: Forward-and-back movement, like a squat, a hinge or a curl.

lengthened: The point in the rep where the working muscle is at its longest while it carries the load. It describes the muscle's length, not whether she feels a stretch.

external rotation: Turning a limb outward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face forward or up, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more to the side. An arm held that way is externally rotated.

shoulder flexion: The arm travelling forward and up in front of the body, all the way to overhead.

upward rotation: The shoulder blade turning so its outer corner rises and the socket tilts upward. It is what lets the arm travel overhead.
