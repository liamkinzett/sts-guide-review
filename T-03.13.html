Fatigue and recovery · T-03.13

What actually ends a set, and what causes the burn

A set ends when she can no longer keep meeting the demand at the standard the exercise requires.

On the surface that can look like several different things:

  • force output drops below what the load needs;
  • her coordination breaks down;
  • pain changes the movement;
  • her grip goes;
  • she stops on purpose before any of that happens;
  • or her technique has drifted too far to keep going.

    • There is no single fatigue chemical behind all of that. A hard set ends because her muscle and her nervous system, together, can no longer keep solving the task. That is why an explanation like "lactate made the muscle fail" is too simple.

    The burn in a hard set is real.

    It is not lactate, and lactate is not what ends the set either. Say the right thing, because the lactic acid story is the one she will have heard.

    Separate three things. Hard, fast fuel use produces a by-product (lactate). Alongside it, acid particles (hydrogen ions) and inorganic phosphate build up in the muscle. Together this build-up is called metabolite accumulation (metabolite accumulation). The acid and the phosphate contribute to the burn. Lactate is the bystander that got the blame.

    One major contributor, physiologically, is calcium handling. Inside a fatigued fibre, the machinery that releases and recovers calcium slows down (calcium handling). As a hard set goes on, less calcium is released, so fewer cross-bridges form, and force drops. It is not that the muscle has run out of fuel; the release mechanism itself is tiring. The slowdown is bigger in fast fibres (type II fibres), which is part of why two clients fail differently on the same set.

    The second half is central. As fibres fatigue, the brain sends more drive and switches on more motor units to compensate (motor unit recruitment). Failure comes when that compensation runs out. Fibre and nervous system together are what end the set. That is why no single chemical explains it. Muscle and growth covers recruitment order.

    Why her reps slow down before she fails. Bar speed usually falls well before the set ends, even when she is trying just as hard. Part of that is mechanical. A muscle produces less force the faster it shortens, which Levers and moment arms covers.

    Part of it is fatigue itself. The fibres already working are becoming less able to produce the same output, so it takes more effort to keep the bar moving. A slowing bar is not proof she is trying less. Often it is the opposite. What matters is whether her technique and position hold up as the set slows, not the bar speed itself.

    Say it like this

    That burn is real, but it isn't lactic acid, and it isn't why you stopped. As the set goes on the working fibres produce less force and your nervous system drives harder to cover for them. The set ends when the whole system can't keep meeting the demand.

    Watch for

    Telling her lactic acid causes soreness the next day. It does not. The next page covers what does.

    Reading a slowing bar as low effort. It is often the opposite: she may be pushing harder than ever. Judge the set by whether technique holds, not by bar speed.

    Reading shaking as fatigue. When she shakes in a position, her brain and her trunk are negotiating stability. She lacks a strategy for holding those joint angles, or she has not spent enough time in the position. Shaking is a stability and exposure problem. Time in the position is the answer, not a lighter load. Practising a lift covers it.

    Heart rate staying up after legs. That is a size argument, not a fatigue one. The page on heart rate after a leg session explains it.

    Terms on this page

    lactate: A by-product of fast fuel use in the muscle. It is not what causes the burn and not what causes soreness.

    metabolite accumulation: The build-up of exercise by-products, chiefly hydrogen ions and inorganic phosphate, inside a working muscle. It contributes to the burn, but it is not the same thing as what ends the set.

    hydrogen ions: The acid particles that build up alongside lactate during hard work. They contribute to the burn.

    calcium handling: Inside the fibre, calcium is released to make the muscle contract and pumped back to let it relax. When that machinery slows, force drops.

    type ii fibres: Fast-twitch muscle fibres. They produce force quickly and tire fast.

    motor unit recruitment: A motor unit is one nerve and the fibres it controls. Recruitment is how many motor units the brain switches on for the task.
