Choosing the equipment

The equipment decides one thing: where the load sits relative to her body. That balance point (centre of mass) is the whole of what it changes.

Think of the squat run backwards. The further behind her the bar sits in a squat, the harder her balance is to control. The further in front of her the load sits in a hinge, the same problem. Every option below is a way of moving the load closer to her.

Switch on a fault, not on her level. A barbell hinge that looks good is a barbell hinge that stays. The switch is for the lifter you have coached, cued and changed things for, and the position still has not moved. It is temporary by design. It is not a beginner-only tool; an advanced lifter can earn the switch just as easily.

The options

# Dumbbell

sub: Load pulled in close, plus a neutral grip

Why it helps: The load sits tight to her body. The grip is the second mechanism, and the one coaches miss. Palms facing in (neutral grip) makes it easier to switch the lats on. The lats are what tie the load through the torso to the pelvis in a hinge. A palms-down dumbbell hold (pronated grip) does the opposite, and it is on the fault list for that reason.

The trade: The load ceiling. Dumbbells run out. When you are managing her overall fatigue rather than her position, that ceiling is the reason to choose them, not a cost.

# Trap bar

sub: A dumbbell's close load with a barbell's loadability

Why it helps: The load sits close, like a dumbbell, and the bar takes as much weight as a barbell. It also self-corrects. When she drifts back into her heels the bar touches her shins, so the most common hinge fault announces itself without a cue.

The trade: Fit. See the watch-out below.

Also: On a glute-biased hinge the trap bar has a strong case. A straight bar sits far enough forward that a glute focus is hard to cue at all. A common split is barbell for hamstring hinges, trap bar or dumbbell for glute hinges, and deficit stiff-leg barbell for adductor hinges. STS still starts her on the barbell and switches when a fault shows.

# Smith machine and shrug machine

sub: The machine tracks the load

Why it helps: External stability. The machine takes over the job of steering the bar. She can keep hinging well when the cost of stabilising has become the limiter.

The trade: The machine holds the bar path, not her spine. Whether that lets a set go closer to failure is decided by the stability test in Effort and intensity. Compounds never go below 1 (RIR) unless that test says otherwise.

Check the trap bar against her frame

An oversized trap bar pushes her hands out toward a snatch-grip width. On a female roster this matters. It is the one case where the straight bar is the better tool. Check the equipment against the lifter before you judge the lifter against the equipment.

Two ladders, and they pull opposite ways.

Compared

columns: External stability | Load ceiling

The path: Barbell, then Smith machine, then shrug machine | Dumbbell, then B-stance dumbbell

Why the fatigue drops: The machine does the stabilising | The equipment caps the weight she can load

What she stabilises: Less | More, not less

The second ladder is the one coaches miss. A B-stance dumbbell RDL asks *more* stabilising of her, not less. It still costs less overall, because the dumbbell caps the load. Fatigue is being managed through absolute load rather than through stability. Both ladders are an intermediate step before the hinge comes out of her program. Use one before you pull the hinge.

Terms on this page

centre of mass: The single point her bodyweight and the load balance around. The further it sits from her feet, the harder she has to work to stay balanced.

neutral grip: Palms facing each other, or facing in toward her body. On a dumbbell RDL it makes the lats easier to switch on.

pronated grip: Palms facing down or away from her, knuckles up. On a dumbbell RDL it lets the shoulders roll forward and the lats switch off.

RIR: Reps in reserve. How many more reps she could have done when the set stopped. 1 RIR means she stopped one rep short of failure.
