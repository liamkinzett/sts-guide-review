Levers and moment arms · T-04.5

How cams, bands and chains change the resistance curve

Cams, bands and chains change what the resistance curve does. A free weight cannot do what any of them do. Know which curve each tool produces and you can judge equipment nobody has written about.

With free weights, gravity acts vertically. The hardest point usually sits where the line of gravity creates the largest moment arm around the joint you care about. To read a free-weight exercise:

  • identify the joint you care about;
  • identify the direction of the external force, which for free weights is downward;
  • look at how far the line of force is from the joint across the range: that distance is the external moment arm;
  • find where that distance is largest. That is where the external torque demand is highest.

    • A dumbbell lateral raise is hardest with the arm level and near-weightless at the bottom, for exactly this reason. The weight a lifter can use is capped by the hardest inch.

    Cables and machines can change this. A cable lets you change the line of pull, making the exercise hardest in a different part of the range. A machine can go further still, depending on its design:

  • it can smooth out the resistance curve;
  • make the exercise hardest at a chosen point;
  • or more closely match a lifter's own strength curve.
    • The tools

    # A cam

    sub: Reshapes the curve by design

    What it does: The shaped wheel on a machine varies the moment arm of the resistance through the range. It deliberately shifts the load to match a target strength curve rather than gravity's own shape.

    # Bands and chains

    sub: Add load as the lifter gets stronger through the lift

    What they do: Bands and chains add load progressively (accommodating resistance) through the rising part of a lift's strength curve. Think squat or bench press, where a lifter is weakest at the bottom and strongest at lockout. They overload the strong top of the lift, where a fixed bar weight is easy. The weak bottom stays possible.

    # A cable

    sub: Keeps the line of pull closer to square through the range

    What it does: The opposite job to bands. A cable can be set up to keep the force closer to perpendicular to the limb through the range. Whether it does depends on where it is anchored and the path the joint takes. Set up well, it is the tool of choice on a pulling pattern whose strength curve falls as the muscle shortens.

    Why it matters. "Does the machine feel even?" and "is the cable version better than the dumbbell?" are both resistance-curve questions. A coach who can name which curve a tool produces can evaluate equipment nobody has written about. That is exactly what the resistance-profile screen in Choosing exercises asks you to do. The best choice between free weights, cables and machines is not ideological. It is the one that best serves the current goal.

    Say it like this

    The band makes the top harder, where you're strongest, without making the bottom impossible. That's the only reason it's on the bar.

    Terms on this page

    resistance curve: How much torque the load places on the joint at each point in the range. It belongs to the exercise: a cam, a cable angle, or gravity on a free weight.

    accommodating resistance: Bands or chains added to a bar so the load rises as she moves through the part of the lift where she is strongest.

    external moment arm: The perpendicular distance between the joint and the line the load pulls along. It belongs to the resistance curve, not to the lifter.
