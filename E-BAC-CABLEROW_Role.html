What it's for

The seated cable row is STS's horizontal pull on continuous cable tension. It carries both biases, lat and upper back, and they are opposite instructions. Every prescription declares which one it is. Choosing without declaring is the reason backs fail to grow.

Compared

columns: Lat-biased | Upper-back-biased

Elbow path: Tucked, close to the side. | Wide, flared well away from the ribs.

Shoulder blade through the set: Held down and locked (depression). | Free. Full travel out and back (protraction) (retraction).

Finish: No hard pinch. Drive toward the hip crease. | Full retraction. Pinch hard.

Bottom: Controlled. The shoulder does not roll forward. | Blades fully out around the ribs to reset the stretch.

Why the tucked elbow works for the lat. On a lat-biased row the cable's line of pull passes straight through the forearm. There is no twisting load at the elbow, and effectively all of the force reaches the lats.

Grip selects the sub-target on an upper-back row. A palms-down grip (pronated grip) with the arm path angled up from the torso reaches traps and rhomboids. A palms-facing grip (neutral grip) with the elbows flaring wider reaches traps. A shoulder-width grip with the palms turned partway in reaches rhomboids.

Seat height is the second dial. Raising the seat and pulling low to high biases the rhomboids further still, with the elbows drawn back on an upward angle. The low-to-high seated row is not a version STS programs as its own exercise; it is a setting on this one.

The single-arm versions are two different jobs.

Single-arm versions

# Same side forward, seated, lat-biased

sub: Same arm and same leg forward (ipsilateral)

Why: The counterforce matches the line of pull. Push hard into the lead leg. Where there is no chest support, brace the off hand on the knee or a bench.

# High to low, bench-supported or half-kneeling, upper-back-biased

sub: The group's one stretched row. The pulley height is what makes it one

Why: Carrying the arm more overhead makes shoulder-blade movement inevitable. An experienced lifter with set habits cannot then compensate through the arm. It is the chest-supported row's next version, aimed at advanced lifters with poor development. Novices are not the target.

Setup: Pulley set high, above the working shoulder. Bench-supported or half-kneeling. Progress opposite knee forward first (contralateral), cross-body and rotational, then same side. Keep the sternum turned toward the side being rowed so the rib cage and the shoulder blade pull apart.

Watch for: The sternum rotating away from the working side. If the sternum moves the spine moves, and the muscle's two ends travel together. That kills both the stretch and the turning force the muscle can produce (torque). Three more to watch. Extending the back. Losing the sideways shift that presses the rib cage into the lat. Reaching so far that the blade turns upward.

The high-to-low version is a teaching tool first

It is a poor stimulus per rep for several weeks, until the variables stop going wrong. Do not count it as a loadable row while it is being learned.

Resistance is continuous, with no dead spot at either end. Which position is loaded follows the bias. The lat setup works the mid-range into extension and depression. The upper-back setup runs the full travel, loading the stretched bottom with the blades out and the shortened finish with the blades in.

Why the slot earns its place. The legs act as an anchor, so she can drive closer to true failure than on a free-weight row. That makes the seated cable row a send-it slot in a session: a loadable exercise that follows a high-skill, low-load opener. Building a session owns the order.

Terms on this page

depression: The shoulder blade pulled down, away from the ear. The opposite of a shrug.

protraction: The shoulder blade sliding forward and out around the rib cage, away from the spine.

retraction: The shoulder blade sliding back toward the spine, so the two blades pinch together.

pronated grip: Palms facing down or away from her, knuckles up. On a row it is the overhand grip.

neutral grip: Palms facing each other, or facing in toward her body. On a row it is the grip on two parallel handles.

ipsilateral: Same side. On a single-arm row it means the rowing arm and the forward leg are on the same side.

contralateral: Opposite side. On a single-arm row it means the forward knee is on the opposite side to the rowing arm.

torque: The turning force on a joint. The further the load sits from the joint, the more of it there is.
