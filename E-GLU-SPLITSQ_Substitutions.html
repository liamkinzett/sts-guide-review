Swaps

The swaps for a split squat form a ladder from easiest to hardest. The order is the useful part. When she can't hold one rung, step down one. When she owns a rung, step up.

The regression ladder, easiest first

Stationary lunge. Bodyweight, both feet on the floor. It is also the test on the When to use it page.

Add external support. A hand on a rack or a pole, so balance stops being the limiter.

Step-down. Genuinely one leg, with the compensations unavailable (see Step-up and step-down).

Goblet-loaded split squat. One weight held at the chest.

Dumbbell split squat, with the dumbbell in the opposite hand (contralateral load). STS programs the opposite-hand version only, never a same-side dumbbell.

Front-foot-elevated split squat.

Rear-foot-elevated split squat, the Bulgarian.

Which rungs you write into a program. Rungs 2, 5, 6 and 7 are the ones STS writes into her program as exercises. Rungs 1 and 3 are not on STS's programmed list, so use them to place her on the ladder, not as prescriptions.

Rung 4, the goblet hold, is programmed as the quad-biased way to hold rungs 6 and 7 rather than as its own exercise. A coach working from the programmed list starts at rung 2.

A deliberate setting, not a regression. The FFE split squat with a negative shin angle (negative shin angle) is used on purpose. It biases her posterior chain (posterior chain). Don't read it as an easier version.

Terms on this page

contralateral load: The weight is held in the hand opposite the working leg.

negative shin angle: Her front shin leans back, so the knee sits behind the ankle rather than out over the toes. It shifts the work away from the front quad and towards the hips and hamstrings.

posterior chain: The muscles down the back of the body that extend the hip and support the spine: glutes, hamstrings and the lower back muscles.
