Variations

STS programs three versions: the leg press with a mid-foot stance, the single leg press, and the hip press. The stance is fixed, so the versions differ by job rather than by foot position.

The programmed versions

# Leg press, mid-foot stance

sub: The default, and the only stance STS uses

What changes: Nothing between sessions, which is the point. What earns the movement its slot is the stimulus-to-fatigue ratio. The machine tracks the load, so she recruits a lot of muscle at a low central cost. Keeping the stance fixed keeps her numbers comparable week to week.

# Single leg press

sub: One leg at a time

What changes: It closes a left-to-right strength gap without loading the spine. The weak side cannot be carried by the strong one.

# Hip press

sub: The knee angle held quiet

What changes: It loads the glutes and hamstrings through hip opening (hip extension) rather than knee bend. Its slot is hip-extension work for a client carrying lower-back fatigue.

Horizontal seated press or 45 degree sled. Both work the same way. A back pad braces the pelvis and spine, which switches off the vigilance a free-standing lift triggers in a less stable lifter. Her effort then goes into the knees and hips instead of balance.

What differs is how the resistance is made. The 45 degree sled loads directly along the incline under gravity. The horizontal press feeds resistance through a cable, cam or weight stack, so the force follows the machine's linkage rather than gravity directly.

Choose between them on availability and her comfort, not on an assumed difference in stimulus. On either machine STS uses the mid-foot stance. Foot placement does change which muscles share the load, but STS does not use it as a lever. When she needs more glute or adductor work, that work is added as its own exercise.

Terms on this page

hip extension: Opening the hip so the thigh travels behind the body line.
