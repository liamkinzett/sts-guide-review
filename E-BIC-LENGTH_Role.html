Biceps · E-BIC-LENGTH#Role

What it's for

The long head of the biceps crosses the shoulder (long head). It can only be loaded at its full length with the arm stretched behind the body. Full length needs the elbow straight and the shoulder extended at the same time (shoulder extension). Only a cable, an incline or a behind-the-back setup reaches it. That is the anatomical reason this slot exists separately from the standing curl.

The profiles differ inside the group (resistance profile), and the difference decides the slotting.

Three profiles in one slot

# Face away and behind-the-back cable curl

sub: The one that loads the fully lengthened long head

Where it is hardest: Mid-to-lengthened (mid-to-lengthened). The cable keeps a lever on the elbow all the way into the stretch.

# Preacher curl

sub: It depends on the implement. That is the whole answer

Where it is hardest: On a conventional shallow bench, a cable preacher peaks in the mid-range and a barbell preacher peaks early, in the elongated range. Both profiles are real. They belong to different tools. Neither grows the elbow flexors more than the other. On a preacher the shoulder is flexed, so the long head is already shortened. Moving the point where the turning force at the elbow peaks (torque) inside a shortened position does not change growth. Pick the implement on availability and comfort. It is not a growth lever.

# Incline dumbbell curl

sub: Loads the mid-range despite the lengthened shoulder position

Where it is hardest: Mid-range. The lever peaks with the forearm at a right angle, whatever the shoulder is doing.

Why cables beat dumbbells here. A dumbbell loses all tension at the bottom. Gravity passes straight through the elbow. A cable keeps a lever on the joint (moment arm) and holds tension in the stretched position.

That applies to the barbell preacher on our setup too. The early, elongated peak belongs to a conventional shallow bench. STS sets the pad steep enough to put the upper arm near vertical. At full extension on that pad, the bar's force runs through the elbow the same way a dumbbell's does. On our setup the cable is the preacher that loads the stretch. Do not read "a barbell preacher peaks in the elongated range" as true of the way we set it up.

A muscle's lengthened position and an exercise's lengthened position are two different things. Failing to tell them apart is a really common coaching fault. The preacher curl is the case that catches people. It stays in this slot, and it is labelled on both axes rather than called "lengthened" without qualification.

Compared

columns: Length position (the muscle) | Load position (the exercise)

On the preacher: Long at the elbow, short at the shoulder. The elbow flexors reach their long length at the bottom. The long head never reaches its full stretch, because the pad holds the shoulder flexed. | Depends on the implement and the pad. A cable preacher peaks mid-range. On a near-vertical pad a dumbbell or a bar loses tension at full extension.

The label: Loaded long, not stretched. | On our setup, the cable is the preacher that loads the bottom.

What a coach must not do is read "preacher curl, lengthened slot" as "stretches the long head". It does not. The preacher and the incline curl are long in different places. That is why the preacher tends to build the lower biceps, near the elbow, more than the incline curl does.

Terms on this page

long head: The head of a muscle that crosses the shoulder joint as well as the elbow: on the triceps it only stretches with the arm raised, on the biceps only with the arm behind the body.

shoulder extension: The upper arm travelling back behind the line of the body.

resistance profile: Where in the range an exercise is hardest and where it is easiest. It is set by the line of the load against the joint, so a dumbbell and a cable can load the same muscle very differently.

mid-to-lengthened: The part of the range from mid-way down to the stretched bottom.

moment arm: The perpendicular distance between the joint axis and the line of force.

torque: The turning force on a joint. The further the load sits from the joint, the more of it there is. It is the force times its moment arm, so the same force can produce very different torque at different joint angles.
