Assessing a new client · T-06.2

Read all seven things before you decide anything

Read all seven things out of her two documents before you decide anything. The order below is the efficient one, because each answer narrows what the next one means.

You will not normally speak to her before the plan is built. She receives the onboarding video and her first program together. So nearly every judgement here is made from what is already written down. If a question is not on the form, you do not have the answer. Work with what is in front of you.

The one exception is a genuine conflict between the two documents on something that changes what you would build. Then you ask.

The seven, in this sequence. Each one sets up the next.

  • Goal
  • Height and bodyweight
  • Training age
  • Available training days
  • Injury history and movement limitations
  • Lifestyle
  • Equipment and gym access

    The seven, one at a time

    # 1. Goal

    sub: The brief. Everything after this points at it.

    Where to read it: On the form, then again in the call summary. The two are often different. The form gets the tidy answer and the call gets the real one. Her timeframe and her previous attempts sit alongside it. A woman who has tried and failed at this twice needs a different first block to one who has never tried.

    Know it off by heart: Not filed in a document you open at check-in time. Every decision you make for her over the next twelve months should trace back to it. If she wants to lose ten kilos, then every phase, every block and every adjustment points at ten kilos. That is true of any goal she brings you.

    Many phases, one endpoint: The road there can be long and it can change direction. A client wanting to lose weight and build her glutes might run a cut, then a hold, then two hypertrophy blocks. Four phases with four different jobs. The plan as a whole still resolves to the one thing she asked for. If you cannot draw a line from the block you are writing back to her goal, you have drifted.

    Decides: Everything. It is the brief the whole strategy is written against.

    # 2. Height and bodyweight

    sub: Two numbers doing two different jobs

    The first job is nutrition: Height and weight are what her estimated maintenance is built from. That calculation, and the targets that come off it, belong to the Nutrition Manual. Take the numbers accurately and pass them on.

    The second job is an extreme: You cannot read body fat off height and weight. A lean muscular woman and an untrained one sit at the same number all the time. What the two figures will do is tell you when someone is a long way from the middle of the curve. That is worth knowing before you write anything.

    Probably underweight: A woman at 160 cm and 48 kg. Assume less tissue, less work capacity and a body that may be more fragile than her enthusiasm suggests. Start conservative on load and volume. Build capacity before you chase intensity. If her form answers also mention missed periods, treat that as a referral conversation rather than a programming one. Missed periods are a primary sign of too little food for what she does (RED-S), not a side note.

    Probably carrying excess weight: A woman at 170 cm and 125 kg. Expect range of motion to be limited in some positions. Expect bodyweight movements like pull-ups and push-ups to be inaccessible early. Lean toward machines and supported variations while she builds. Check that the equipment you are programming actually fits her.

    In the middle: Anything that does not stand out will not change your programming. That is the point of the question. It is not there to classify her. It is there to catch either end of the bell curve before it catches you.

    The real composition read: It happens at the end of priming, when her first check-in photos arrive. Everyone primes first regardless, and the decision it feeds comes after priming ends. Nothing is waiting on it.

    Decides: Nutrition maintenance, which the Nutrition Manual owns. Programming only at the extremes.

    # 3. Training age

    sub: How long she takes to recover and adapt. Not years in a gym.

    Where to read it: The form gives you three answers to work from. Her training experience. Her current training situation (structure, split, frequency). Her current training variables (volume, intensity, frequency, tempo, rest).

    Read the specificity: Read it as hard as you read the content. A woman who tells you her exact working sets and where she has stalled is telling you something a year count never will. Vague answers are themselves a signal. The classification pages later in this topic give you the full method and the table.

    At intake: This is one overall assumption. You have not seen her move, so you cannot break it down by pattern yet. That refinement comes through priming.

    Decides: Volume, the progression model, and how far you can trust her effort ratings.

    # 4. Available training days

    sub: The form asks which days she prefers. That is her best case.

    Read it against her schedule: The form covers her occupation, her work hours, her rough daily routine and her regular commitments. A woman who says she prefers five days but works twelve hour shifts is telling you two different things. Build for the number she will hit in a bad week rather than a good one. A four day program she completes beats a five day program she abandons every third week.

    When she gives you a range: Plenty of clients answer "three to four days". Build the three. She earns the fourth by hitting the three consistently first.

    When her week is unpredictable: A mother with young kids is the obvious case. Write the three day split with an optional fourth session on top. This is not a compromise. It is the better structure for her life.

    The rule for an optional session: All the main progression lives in the core days. Every primary lift, every progression scheme, every load increase you are tracking sits inside the three she will definitely hit. The optional fourth session adds volume to focus areas and nothing else. It complements the block rather than carrying any part of it. Get that wrong and the optional session stops being optional. If her squat progression lives in session four, a bad week costs her the thing the whole block was built on. Write it so that missing the fourth session costs her nothing structural.

    Decides: The split, and where the progression is allowed to live.

    # 5. Injury history and movement limitations

    sub: Anything that changes what she can load and how

    Where to read it: The form asks this at exercise level, which particular exercises aggravate it. Read that carefully. Read the rehab answer too.

    Already with a physio: A client already working with a physio has a plan you should be supporting rather than competing with.

    Decides: Exercise selection and any substitutions, before she ever sees the program.

    # 6. Lifestyle

    sub: Stress, sleep and work decide how much training she can absorb

    Where to read it: The form covers all three in detail across its lifestyle and sleep sections. What you are reading for is recovery capacity. A woman sleeping five hours on rotating shifts does not get the same volume as one sleeping eight on a fixed schedule. Their training ages do not change that.

    Why sleep weighs so much: Short sleep cuts performance by around 7.6% overall, and lower body work is hit harder than upper. A single night of restricted sleep drops muscle protein synthesis by about 18% and testosterone by about 24%, and raises cortisol by about 21%. Poor sleep is not a lifestyle footnote. It changes what she can absorb.

    Holidays and events: Check her upcoming holidays and events as well. It is easier to plan a deload around a wedding than to salvage a block after one.

    Decides: How aggressive the volume and the phase length can be.

    # 7. Equipment and gym access

    sub: What she can actually get her hands on

    Where to read it: The form does not ask this directly. Assume a commercial gym unless something in her answers says otherwise.

    Do this: Only write a substitution where the equipment is genuinely obscure. A pendulum squat, a belt squat or a hip press are worth a sub in the session notes. Most of what you program will be there. Use your judgement on the rest.

    A home gym or travel: Where her answers say she trains at home, or travels for work, Training around life covers a week where the equipment has changed.

    Why it matters: Session notes only work if she reads them. Fill them with substitutions for equipment every gym owns and she will stop reading the ones that count.

    Decides: Whether the program you designed is the program she can run.

    Why the order. Each answer narrows what the next one means. Goal sets the destination. Height and weight flag an extreme, if there is one. Training age shapes the route. Days, injuries, lifestyle and equipment decide what is actually buildable. Read them out of order and you will often design a program twice.

    Having all seven in front of you before you decide is not optional. The order is advice. It is the efficient sequence, not a sacred one.

    Watch for

    Reading days-available first and letting it drive everything. It is the most concrete number on the form, so it pulls the eye early. It is a constraint rather than a goal. It shapes the container, not the contents.

    Terms on this page

    RED-S: Relative energy deficiency in sport. She is eating too little for what she does, and her body starts shutting things down. Missed periods are one of the first signs.
