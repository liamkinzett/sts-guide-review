When to use it

The problem you notice. Her row sets end with her standing up out of the hinge, not with her back failing. Her low back or her core gives out first, every set, and the back muscles never get near failure. The chest-supported row takes the low back and the hips out of the row. What is left is the shoulder blade moving on the rib cage.

Reach for it when

# Her set ends with her standing up out of the hinge, not with her back failing

sub: The low back is the limiter on every free-weight row

Confirm it: Watch the last reps of her barbell or dumbbell row. She rises out of the hinge before the pull slows.

What it gets her: The back worked to failure instead of the low back. Chest support removes the stabiliser bottleneck, so she can put maximum force into the target.

# You want the rhomboids loaded, and loaded heavily

sub: The mid-back between the blades is the target

Confirm it: The bias is upper back and the goal is load, not learning.

What it gets her: The most stable and most loadable rhomboid row STS has. The position variables are stripped out and only the shoulder blade's movement is left.

# A dip is too much for her, but you still need to read her shoulder extension

sub: An assessment at a load she can hold

Confirm it: She cannot yet do a dip, or a dip provokes her shoulder.

What it gets her: The same read at a load she can hold. A chest-supported row shows whether the shoulder blade dumps over the top. Or it shows she can take the arm behind her, with the blade and collarbone moving back and the shoulder rotating in.

# She rounds over the pad and you want mid-back thickness rather than lower-back work

sub: The upper back curls over the pad on every rep

Confirm it: From the side, her upper back is rounded over the pad and her chest is off it.

What it gets her: Load moved off the spinal muscles (erectors) and onto the rhomboids and mid-traps, by holding the chest proudly lifted for the whole set.

It is expensive, and the audit signal is not a set count

Chest-supported rows carry a high whole-body fatigue cost (central fatigue). Many more sets of single-arm cable pulldowns are tolerable than of chest-supported rows.

When she reports the working feel has moved to her rear delts and biceps, the productive sets are over. That is the junk-volume audit. You will not see it on video; she has to tell you. See Volume.

What the pad does, and what it does not. External stability is what makes the movement teachable, and it is why this family is a starting point rather than a destination. But a pad is not a coach.

An experienced lifter with set habits can still compensate through the arm on a chest-supported row. That is why the bench-supported overhead-arm cable version exists (see Seated cable row). And a machine gives her none of the self-stabilised shoulder function the pull-up builds. Name the goal, then pick the tool.

Setup. Seat so the arms extend fully in front, chest proud on the pad, arm path set before the load. Setup has the full list.

Terms on this page

erectors: The long muscles either side of the spine that hold the back straight and extend it. Rounding over a pad stretches and loads them instead of the mid-back.

central fatigue: Tiredness in the nervous system and the whole body, not in one muscle. It is why some exercises cost far more than their set count suggests.
