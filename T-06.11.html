Assessing a new client · T-06.11

Her goal sets the emphasis. There are three paths

Every client gets stronger and every client builds muscle. What her path decides is which of the two you protect when a programming decision could go either way.

It is easy to read the three paths as separate systems. They are not. Strength and size are not mutually exclusive, and no path gives up one to get the other.

The three paths

# 1. Powerlifting strength

sub: For the client who wants to squat more weight or add to her total

The base: Squat, bench and deadlift. Every accessory is chosen to develop those three further.

Programming character: Lower volume, longer rest on the heavy compounds, no compound work taken to failure. Load goes up only once she is moving sub-maximal weight well.

# 2. Powerbuilding strength

sub: The middle path, and where a lot of clients sit

The base: Progressing the key lifts is still the main focus, usually the big three plus a pull-up and a strict press.

The accessories: They split two ways. Some target a specific weakness in those main lifts. The rest add training volume to an area she wants developed, most often glutes.

Who she is: She wants her numbers to go up and she wants to look like she trains.

# 3. Hypertrophy

sub: Building muscle in the areas she wants it

The base: Exercise selection follows what grows the target rather than what carries over to a lift.

Programming character: Fewer exercises with more sets on each, a 6–20 rep core and every session logged. Set-extending techniques (intensifiers) are used selectively in hypertrophy blocks, then removed in cuts.

Why it works. Adaptation is specific to what you ask the body for. Heavy work in low rep ranges is the most efficient route to maximal strength. Moderate loads taken close to failure are the most efficient route to growth. Heavy loads build more maximal strength than light ones, and maximal strength tracks the load used.

Why they are emphases, not categories. Strength improves across a wide span of rep ranges. Growth happens across a wide span of loads, from roughly 30% to 85% of her one rep max. Two conditions: volume is matched, and the sets are taken close enough to failure. Close enough means nought to three reps in reserve (RIR).

"Close enough to failure" is not one distance. Failure is critical at light loads and optional at heavy ones. Around 30% she has to go to genuine failure for the set to count. At 80% and above she gets the same growth stopping one to three reps short.

How close to failure a set must get before it counts

0 | At light loads, around 30% of her max, only genuine failure counts

3-1 | At 80% and above, stopping one to three reps short grows the same

So light work is not a softer option. It is a harder one with a narrower margin. That is worth knowing before you write it for a client who dislikes heavy sets. And the hypertrophy client still adds weight to the bar, while the powerlifting client still grows.

Fat loss is not a fourth path. It is a nutritional context, and any of the three can run inside it. A client cutting on the powerlifting path stays on the powerlifting path.

She can still get stronger in a deficit. Strength is not only a product of tissue. Skill keeps improving, and intelligent meal timing protects session quality. Progress stays available even when growth mostly is not. What she is unlikely to do is add much muscle. You are protecting what she has while she gets better at expressing it.

Where fat loss is the whole goal, program with the hypertrophy path in mind. The conditions do not favour building, so the job becomes retention. The programming style itself does not change.

Key figure

value: 10–15

unit: hard sets per muscle per week inside a cut

note: When fat loss is the whole goal, regardless of her training age

When her goal straddles two. Plenty do. Powerbuilding is usually the answer, because it is built for exactly that client. When her goal changes, the path changes with it, rather than waiting for a phase to end.

Watch for

Programming a path she did not choose. A client who came to you for her glutes does not want three quarters of her week servicing a bench press. However much better her bench would get. Read the goal, pick the path, then let the path make the decisions you would otherwise make on instinct.

Terms on this page

intensifiers: Techniques that make a set harder than a plain straight set: drop sets, rest-pause, partials past failure and the like.

RIR: Reps in reserve. How many more reps she could have done when the set stopped. Zero RIR is failure.
