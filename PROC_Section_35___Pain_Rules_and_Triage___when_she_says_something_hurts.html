What to do when she says something hurts

This page adds nothing. It is this topic's rules in the order you use them, in the session where she says it.

Train around it, or refer

? Does it feel like the set working (discomfort) rather than a warning (pain)?

yes: The set continues. Nothing below applies.

? Back or leg pain plus any cauda equina flag, started in the last two weeks?

yes: Emergency department today. Skip every other step.

? Anything on the stop-and-refer table, seven out of ten consistently, or still there at three days to a week?

yes: The session's plan ends. Refer, and change the plan.

= Sort her movements into three buckets. Train the palliative and no-effect ones. Queue the rest, ranked.

Route two look-alikes first

A movement felt in the wrong place, with no pain in it. Faults and what you can actually see (its Tier 3 block). That is a data signal, not a pain report. The moment pain enters it, it comes back here.

A cramp mid-set. The set ends. Check her setup before her hydration.

Step 1. Test. The first question is never how bad. It is which of two things it is. Do not ask for a number yet. Ask whether it feels like the set working or like a warning. Direction separates them, not intensity: discomfort resolves into an adaptation, pain resolves into damage. A burn that builds across the rep is the stimulus. A sharp catch is not, however mild.

If it is discomfort, the set continues and nothing below applies. If it is pain, keep going. And listen while she answers. The cauda equina flags do not arrive as an emergency. They arrive as a passing comment about her leg feeling weird or about the toilet. Back or leg pain plus any of them, with onset inside two weeks, skips every step below and goes straight to step 5, today.

Step 2. Read. A working read, never a diagnosis. Name your best guess, label it a guess, never grade it. No palpating, no measuring, no test that needs your hands on the joint. The disclaimer does not change what you did.

You are not working out what is wrong, you are working out what changed. Onset and mechanism. The whole injury history, not just the presenting joint. Then context asked directly: a deficit, a stress period, a changed split. The three commonest answers are all things a coach can fix. The program changed. The recovery changed. Life changed.

And do not grade severity today. The clock runs in both directions. Twenty-four hours is too early to conclude anything. What feels surgical on day one can settle by day three. What feels fine the next day can worsen on days three and four.

Step 3. Do. The middle state has a method and it needs no diagnosis, which is why it is yours.

Do, in order

Sort everything she does into three buckets: palliative, no effect, provocative. Ask her, then watch her.

Train the palliative and no-effect buckets. That is the whole session. It is the thing that makes her better, not a holding pattern.

Where a movement has to stay in, work the lever order. Position, then range to what is painless, then load, then the exercise, then stop.

Follow the mechanism when you pick the lever. The wrong regression makes it worse: low-bar for a shear mechanism, a deficit pull for a deadlift that hurts off the floor.

Say the why out loud. Showing her what provokes and what relieves is your biggest lever on the fear component of her pain.

What you do not do at any point: palpate, measure or grade anything. You describe what you saw and what she reported. That description is genuinely valuable and nobody else has it.

Step 4. Change. What moves in the program:

  • The buckets become the program. The provocative list becomes a ranked queue and the ranking is the order movements come back. Re-test the top of the queue after a block of palliative work.
  • Her calories do not go down. A client who gets injured and cuts food is trying to heal in a deficit.
  • Progress is read off her day rather than the bar, and you have to re-ask. She will not volunteer that stairs stopped hurting.
  • The week she feels ready, the exercise caps the load rather than her intent. Recurrence is a programming failure. She will feel good before she is good.

    • What explicitly does not change: everything outside the provocative bucket keeps training. And the training-through ceilings do not transfer to her. The five and the three belong to a diagnosed client with clearance. A client without a diagnosis is not in the population those numbers describe. Her case runs on the buckets.

    Step 5. Escalate. This step is the topic. The third decision, does she stop and see someone, is the one you must never sit on.

    Red flags

    title: Step 5. Where it goes and when

    Back or leg pain plus any cauda equina flag, onset inside two weeks: emergency department the same day. Refer on the symptom. Do not wait for a sign. You are not the one who rules it out

    Anything on the stop-and-refer table. Joint pain plus a sign. Instability or night pain on their own. Tingling, numbness or weakness that is not fatigue, in any limb. Pelvic floor symptoms. Persistent absent periods. Anything acute. The session's plan ends and the conversation starts, and the table names the practitioner type

    Seven out of ten reported consistently, or still consistent between three days and one week: escalate. These are referral thresholds, not the training ceilings, and seven must not leak into that decision

    A medication question, including everything that sounds like scheduling: the prescriber, named specifically. Keep coaching everything else

    emergency: Chest pain, collapse, fainting, a seizure, breathing problems, stroke signs or uncontrollable bleeding: triple zero. A knock to the head and anaphylaxis have their own pages in this topic.

    Two boundaries survive every escalation. You refer and change the plan, rather than programming around the thing you referred for. And what you hand over is a description of what you saw and when it started. A working read may sit alongside it, labelled a guess and never graded. It never replaces the description. You may challenge a years-old ban with the two-things question. You never lift a medical restriction.

    The emergency list is When to call triple zero. Do not read the stop-and-refer table as one. That table says when to send her to a clinician. It does not say when to call an ambulance. The two are different decisions on different clocks. Cauda equina has its own page and covers cauda equina only. A coach who believes a client needs emergency care still acts on that judgement.

    Once a surgeon's or physio's clearance letter is in hand, read it the way any clinician's report is read: behaviourally. Which movements are restricted, which are cleared. Never a stage or a grade taken from the letter itself. That is where the specifics for a discharged joint-replacement client come from, not from a protocol.
