Building her delts without wrecking her shoulders

You are writing her pressing day and you want delts that grow without a shoulder that complains. This page is the Shoulders topic's exercise pages in the order you use them. Nothing here is new doctrine.

1 · Test: place her on the stability ladder before you pick the press

Standing is earned, never assumed. The vertical press runs up a ladder of stability: supported incline, then seated overhead, then half-kneeling, then standing on both feet. Her early load goes on the rungs where the bench or the machine holds her position (externally stabilised). She moves up a rung when she owns the one below it.

Check the prerequisite before any of it. She must be able to hold her lower back still while her arm travels overhead (shoulder flexion). Without that, the low back supplies the range and the press trains the wrong thing. The test is the overhead reach: rib cage over pelvis, then the shoulder blade turning upward while staying down, then the joint finishing the reach. Overhead press, Watch for, has it in full.

If she reports pain, ask for video before you change anything in the program. See Overhead press and Faults and what you can actually see.

2 · Read: three things this topic teaches you to see

What you see, and what it means

# A lower back that arches to finish an overhead press

sub: Usually a shoulder fault, not a bracing fault

What it means: The shoulder blade will not turn upward far enough to get the arm overhead (upward rotation). The lower back supplies the rest. Screening it as a bracing problem sends you to the wrong joint. See Overhead press.

# Shrugging on a raise

sub: A range signal

What it means: She has run out of range where tension is still being added. End the rep there. See Lateral raise.

# A clean machine press

sub: Stimulus, never evidence

What it means: Take the machine for the stimulus it gives. Do not read a clean machine press as proof she can press a free weight overhead. See Overhead press.

Also check which rib-cage fault she actually has before you cue either. A sternum stuck down is the opposite of ribs flaring up. See Lateral raise.

3 · Do: the topic's own pairing logic, in order

  • Press for the vertical arc. Cable lateral raise for the horizontal arc. The pairing is deliberate and the two implements are opposites. The cable is hardest from the stretched bottom to mid-range. The dumbbell is hardest at the top. They complement each other; they do not substitute for each other. See Lateral raise.
  • Buy the elbow tuck mechanically. A narrower grip forces the elbows in rather than relying on the cue. Flaring the elbows out to full (abduction) jams the upper arm bone against the bony roof of the shoulder. See Overhead press.
  • Set the arm path in the scapular plane, from her rib-cage shape. Slightly forward of straight out to the side (scapular plane). Raising at a right angle to the body regardless of where her arm sits is where lateral raise impingement lives. See Lateral raise.
  • Set the cable height per lifter. The criterion is written down: the cable runs perpendicular to the forearm at the bottom of the rep. One cable height per session is the working limit. See Lateral raise.
  • Rear delt on a cable, matched to her fibre direction. Shoulder blade held slightly forward (protraction) and the work in the mid range. Never force the hands further back than the rear delt can actively shorten. See Rear delt.
  • Do not add front raises for size. For anyone already pressing heavy they are redundant volume. Programming one is a Choosing exercises decision and rarely the right one for size. See Front raise.

    • 4 · Change: the ladder, not the deletion

    Where a movement provokes the shoulder, move down the vertical press ladder until a rung loads without provoking it. Keep the rest of the session intact. See Overhead press.

    On the raises, change the pulley height rather than the exercise. The correct height is individual anyway. See Lateral raise and Rear delt.

    Two levers are not swaps at all. Slow the tempo, or run the lateral raises before the press so the same perceived effort arrives at a lower absolute load (pre-exhaust). In the worked case the overhead press came out of the session and the rear delt fly stayed in, unchanged.

    What does not change: her load figures (Effort and intensity), her tempo (Tempo and pauses), and the order call between the press and the raises (Building a session).

    5 · Escalate: where the topic hands over

    The front raise ladder ends in stopping the exercise. Past that point it is Pain rules and when to refer. A clinical injury goes to a practitioner. This is not medical advice.

    Two shoulder-health requests you will hear, and where they go. Y raises are rejected: if she can already get into the Y position, the lower trap was never the limit. Rotator cuff work is not programmed to rotate a dumbbell overhead: the cuff's job is to keep the joint in place. Region by region holds both decisions and what replaces them.

    Three things a coach does here rather than looks up. Choose between the seated and the standing press on stability, never on which grows more muscle. Coach the machine shoulder press to the overhead press's standards; it has no seat height, handle or grip standard of its own. And do not write a resistance profile for the front raise beyond what its page states.

    Keep the nuance in proportion. The fine cable heights and fibre alignments in this topic are for advanced physique athletes, where small changes in proportion count. A general fitness client needs only to pull back and work the muscle. Do not escalate her program into physique-athlete detail.

    Terms on this page

    externally stabilised: The machine, bench or support holds her position so she does not have to.

    shoulder flexion: The arm travelling forward and up in front of the body, all the way to overhead.

    upward rotation: The shoulder blade turning so its outer corner rises and the socket tilts upward. It is what lets the arm travel overhead.

    abduction: A limb travelling out and away from the midline of the body: the arm in a lateral raise, or the thigh moving out to the side.

    scapular plane: The arm path slightly forward of straight out to the side, in line with the angle her shoulder blade sits at on her rib cage.

    protraction: The shoulder blade sliding forward and out around the rib cage, away from the spine.

    pre-exhaust: Tiring one muscle with an isolation exercise first, so that it is the limiter in the compound lift that follows.
