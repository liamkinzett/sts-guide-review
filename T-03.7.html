Fatigue and recovery · T-03.7

Cold water immersion straight after training

Getting into cold water straight after lifting reduces the muscle built from that session.

The effect is small and consistent, and it is in the direction of harm. Cold water is optional, which is what makes it worth avoiding rather than worth worrying about.

An ice bath or a cold plunge after training is cold water immersion (cold water immersion).

The window. Cold applied within 20 minutes of training suppresses muscle protein building (muscle protein synthesis) for up to 5 hours. It blunts the growth signal in the muscle (mTOR). Over time it also reduces the activity of the cells that donate new nuclei to the fibre (satellite cells).

Strength and power are blunted too. This is not a size-only cost.

The sharpest version. Trained men lifted for twelve weeks. Those who did easy active recovery after sessions gained muscle nuclei (myonuclei), grew their fast fibres (type II fibres) and produced more work. Those who got into cold water gained none of the three.

What to tell her. Timing is the lever. The harm is attached to cold applied close to the session. This rule does not say she may never get in cold water. Ice on an injury is a different question, and Pain rules and when to refer owns it.

Say it like this

If you love the cold plunge, keep it. Just keep it well away from training. Straight after a session it takes a little off what the session built.

Terms on this page

cold water immersion: Sitting in cold water after training. An ice bath or a cold plunge.

muscle protein synthesis: The process that builds new muscle protein after training. It is the repair and growth signal a session is meant to switch on.

mtor: The signalling pathway inside the muscle that switches on protein building after training.

satellite cells: Cells that sit on the surface of a muscle fibre and help it repair and grow.

myonuclei: The nuclei inside a muscle fibre, its control centres. More of them let the fibre grow bigger.

type ii fibres: Fast-twitch muscle fibres. They produce force quickly and tire fast.
