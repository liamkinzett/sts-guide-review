Why do reps slow down as a set goes on?

A bar that slows across a set is a normal trade-off doing its job. It is not a fault, and it is not a failure of effort. Do not stop a set because the bar slowed. Do not correct her for it either.

Push an empty shopping trolley and it is easy to get it moving fast. Load it right up with bags and pushing just as hard barely picks up speed. The trolley is not broken, and the person pushing is not failing: a heavier load simply cannot move as fast, however hard it is pushed. A muscle works the same way. As the load it carries climbs, the speed it can move that load at falls, whatever the effort behind it.

Three names cover the phases of a rep. The lift, where the muscle shortens, is concentric (concentric). A still hold, with no joint movement, is isometric. The lowering, where the muscle lengthens under load, is eccentric.

The mechanism. A muscle trades force for speed (force-velocity relationship). The faster it shortens, the less force it can produce. A light load moves fast. As the load climbs toward a lifter's max, speed falls toward zero, which is a hold (isometric).

On the back squat, bar speed runs from about 0.97 m/s at 20% of max down to 0.54 m/s at 90%. Those figures illustrate the mechanism. Neither is a prescription.

Lowering produces more force than lifting. A muscle forced to lengthen under load (eccentric) produces meaningfully more force than one that holds or shortens. It does so at a lower energy cost. The direction is what a coach uses. Do not quote a percentage for the size of that margin. You will hear 20 to 50 percent. STS does not use that figure, and neither do you.

On the floor. This is why bar speed slows across a set as fatigue builds. Recruitment is also rising at the same time, as the last page covers. Both changes go together in a hard set, and both push speed the same way. A slowing bar is not a clean gauge of either one alone.

The slowing is the mechanism working as expected. Effort and intensity owns the measured within-set slowdown and uses it as the audit of effort, not as a recruitment meter.

What this means for coaching. A coach who understands the trade-off reads a slowing bar correctly. Treating every slowdown as a fault, or a reason to stop, costs her. It strips a set of its last productive reps, or teaches her to fear ordinary fatigue.

Watch for

Describing the shape of the curve to her. Whether force falls away in a curve or a straight line is not something a coach needs. Say the plain mechanism: force falls as speed rises, and lowering beats lifting for force.

Applying any of these figures to her as fact. Every one was worked out in men or in isolated muscle. Hold them as illustration.

Say it when the bar slows

That's not you backing off. As the set gets harder, the fibres already working fatigue and your nervous system drives harder to keep the weight moving. The bar slowing is expected. It doesn't tell us which part of that caused it.

Terms on this page

concentric: The lifting half of a rep, where the muscle shortens under load.

force-velocity relationship: The trade-off between how much force a muscle can produce and how fast it can shorten. More speed means less force.

isometric: A hold. The muscle works against the load but the joint does not move.

eccentric: The lowering half of a rep, where the muscle lengthens under load.
