What it's for

The overhead press is STS's vertical press and the front delt's main loaded pattern. It is hardest at the bottom, with the front delt stretched, and the elbow tuck is what buys that range. The seated dumbbell version has a range large enough to work all three heads of the delt.

What the joint is doing. The arm travels forward and up to overhead (shoulder flexion) with the upper arm turned slightly out (external rotation). It is the mirror image of the dip. What the pattern trains is shoulder flexion, external rotation and the shoulder blade turning upward (upward rotation), all over a rib cage that stays stacked.

Seated is the default and standing is earned. The choice between them is a stability decision, not a size decision. The ladder runs supported incline, then seated overhead, then half-kneeling, then standing on both feet. Her early load goes on the rungs where the bench holds her (externally stabilised). Standing, where her own trunk holds the position (internally stabilised), is something she earns.

The seat is doing a job. Make it pay. Sitting stabilises the pelvis and spine and removes the demand to brace everything at once. A coach who buys that stability and then does not push the output has spent the constraint for nothing.

The standing press has one fault to look for before any other. As she fatigues she supplies the last of the range from her lower back arching, not from the shoulder (lumbar finish). Read it as a shoulder-flexion fault, not a bracing fault. A lumbar finish here is usually a shoulder fault explains.

External stability answers two different questions. Know which one you are asking. Added stability standardises the bottom of the rep and makes training to failure safe when she is alone. That is a stimulus argument.

But two arms, then a seat, then a Smith machine each strip away a signal about what she can control. That is a diagnosis argument. "Stability equals output" is the wrong reason to reach for a machine. Take it for the stimulus, and never read it as evidence she can press.

The three versions STS programs

# Seated dumbbell overhead press

sub: The free-weight rung, and often the first one she tolerates

Why: Range large enough to work all three heads. The load is self-limiting, because she has to get the dumbbells into position.

# Barbell strict press

sub: The loadable rung, and the only one with a real strength ceiling

Why: That is its whole difference. The hands are fixed, so the grip sets the elbow tuck mechanically rather than by cue. The bar has to clear the face, which is why the first rep is run slowly. The load is no longer self-limiting the way a dumbbell she has to kick up is.

Also: STS coaches it from the rack, seated. Pins set to touch the bar lightly at her true bottom range. Hips slid a little forward on the seat so the shoulder path is not blocked. Every rep controlled back to a dead stop on the pins. It is also the answer for a client training alone to failure: she dumps the bar onto the pins instead of being pinned under it.

# Machine shoulder press

sub: The most externally stabilised rung, and better than its reputation

Why: The argument for it is a prerequisite one, not a stimulus one. Most clients do not yet own the stability an effective free overhead press demands. The machine supplies it and the overhead range gets trained anyway.

Also: Take it where getting her overhead matters more than what she gets overhead with. It is also the rung to regress to rather than abandoning the pattern. It has no seat height, handle or grip standard of its own: coach it to this page's standards.

Who gets the barbell, and what she does with it. Choosing exercises decides who has earned an internally stabilised press. Effort and intensity decides what she does with the load. As a compound, the press holds at one rep in reserve (RIR) at its hardest and never crosses it. Tempo and pauses decides the tempo.

The slot. Normally the first press of a push day, because it is the skill-heavy free-weight movement. But order by what is lagging. Whatever is trained first gets the highest neural drive. Where the side or rear delts are the lagging heads, the raises come first and the press last. That call belongs to Building a session.

There is a second slot and it is not a size one. The standing single-arm dumbbell overhead press is a test, not a builder (gatekeeper exercise). Its three constraints, single arm, standing and overhead, are the point. Remove any of them and the signal is gone. STS doesn't program the standing single-arm dumbbell overhead press as a working exercise; use it to see what she owns.

Terms on this page

shoulder flexion: The arm travelling forward and up in front of the body, all the way to overhead.

external rotation: Turning a limb outward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face forward or up, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more to the side. An arm held that way is externally rotated.

upward rotation: The shoulder blade turning so its outer corner rises and the socket tilts upward. It is what lets the arm travel overhead.

externally stabilised: The machine, bench or support holds her position so she does not have to.

internally stabilised: Her own trunk and bracing hold the position under load.

RIR: Reps in reserve. How many more reps she could have done when the set stopped.

gatekeeper exercise: A movement chosen to show you whether she owns a position, not to build muscle. Remove its constraints and it shows you nothing.

lumbar finish: Finishing a hip movement by arching the lower back instead of extending the hip. On an overhead press the same arch supplies shoulder range instead.
