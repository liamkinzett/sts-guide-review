Reading her training data · T-39.3

Signals that change a program, and signals that do not

Six things in a logbook change a program. Six things that feel like they should change it do not, on their own. Learn both columns before you read a single check-in.

Changes the programChanges nothing on its own
Two flat exposures on one movementA single bad session
A decline, once read rather than reacted toSoreness, in either direction
Technique degrading within a setA missed session
Effort ratings that the repeat-set test contradictsA pump, or the absence of one
A reported change in where she feels a movementBodyweight moving in one week
Sleep that has genuinely changedHer own request for variety, on its own

The left column, in plain words.

Two flat (exposures) on one movement: the same lift has not moved for two sessions in a row, inside the same block. Progression owns the read. It is a trigger to look, not yet to change.

A decline, once read: a number going down has several possible causes, and only one is genuine regression. Progression has the list. You read it against the list before you touch anything.

Technique degrading within a set: the last reps look different from the first. Faults and what you can actually see owns what counts as a fault.

Effort ratings the (repeat-set test) contradicts: she logged a set as one rep short, rested sixty seconds, and comfortably repeated it. Her effort numbers are not yet readable. Effort and intensity owns the fix.

A reported change in where she feels a movement: she says the hip thrust has moved into her hamstrings. That is data, not chat. Faults and what you can actually see has the questions to ask.

Sleep that has genuinely changed: a real shift in her sleep, not one poor night. Fatigue and recovery treats sleep as a training variable.

The right column, in plain words. A missed session is not a session to make up. Training around life owns it, and the week's job was a stimulus, not a total. Soreness is not a measure of anything, in either direction. Fatigue and recovery has the reason. One week of bodyweight is noise. A request for variety is a request, and it is answered in Motivation and adherence, not in the program.

The right column is the harder half. Every item in it produces a feeling that something should be done. Doing something is how a program stops being a program.

Two worked reads. Her leg press was flat on Monday. One flat session is a bad night, a poor warm-up or a week of bad sleep. Nothing changes. Her leg press was flat on Monday and again on Thursday, in the same block. That is two exposures. Now you go and look.

She logged a hard week and is sore everywhere, and the scale is up a kilo since last check-in. Soreness is not a measure of anything, and one week of bodyweight is noise. Nothing changes.

Watch for

Reacting to the most recent session because it is the most recent. A week is not a trend.

Terms on this page

exposures: The number of separate sessions in a week in which a muscle is trained. A stall is counted in them, never in calendar weeks.

repeat-set test: She rests sixty seconds and repeats a set she called one rep short, at the same weight. If she comfortably does, the set was not as hard as she logged.
