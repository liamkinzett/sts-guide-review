Muscle and growth · T-01.15

Where in the range does a hold grow the most muscle?

In most cases, a hold at a long muscle length grows more than one at a short length.

If an isometric hold is being used for growth, muscle length appears to matter. A hold means the muscle works while the joint stays still (isometric). This is a narrower question than biasing a moving rep toward the stretch, which the next page covers. This one asks about a hold: one joint angle, held still, long against short.

The gap between the two varies, and on one muscle it reversed. Use it where it is free. Do not extend it to her compound lifts.

What the direct comparisons show. In four of five, the long position grew more. In one the two lengths came within a point of each other. In one the short-length hold produced no growth at all. On the hamstring, the upper part grew more from the short hold, while its middle and lower parts favoured long by a small margin.

Why it does not settle anything harder. Every comparison used untrained people. Every one ran six to 12 weeks. Every one held a single joint angle still rather than moving through a range.

The holds ran 5 to 15 seconds, at 50 to 70% or 60 to 80% of a maximal effort. One used a light 30% hamstring curl. None was a multi-joint barbell movement. Do not extend the size of the effect to a trained client or to a compound lift.

What this does not touch. It does not change the rule that muscle length is a bad predictor of where a movement is hard. That is about the turning force on the joint (torque) in a moving rep, in Levers and moment arms. Hold the two apart.

What this means for coaching. Where the movement calls for a long-length hold, this is real backing to lean on. Tempo and pauses decides where a pause goes.

Terms on this page

isometric: A hold. The muscle works against the load but the joint does not move.

torque: The turning force on a joint. The further the load sits from the joint, the more of it there is.
