Ankle pain, continued: read, and what to do

Step 2. Read

Take the whole injury history, and ask by region explicitly. "Any injuries? Hips, knees, ankles?" Clients systematically under-report. The worked example runs the other way and is worth knowing. An old ankle sprain was the upstream cause of low back pain in a standing overhead press. "My ankle thing caused my low back thing doing an overhead thing."

Then pain or discomfort. Then the two numbers: seven out of ten reported consistently, or still consistent between three days and one week.

Read the twelve months before the session, not the session

Four things put people here. Under-preparation going into a block. Under-preparation coming back from injury. Training through an existing injury. Accumulated workload with no real off-season. Any injury changes the demands her training puts on her, and the detraining that follows is what sets up the next one. A broken hand can precede a lower-limb tear for exactly that reason.

That list comes from elite male athletes. The list transfers. Their numbers do not, and none are printed here.

First lever by presentation

# Achilles pain that is new, swollen and angry

sub: Refer first

Do this: Only once she is assessed and cleared does the stage apply. Then holds, unload, change the joint angle. Not lowering-only work, not plyometrics.

# Long-standing, quiet, thickened Achilles, already diagnosed

sub: Load it

Do this: Heavy slow resistance, the version she will actually do. The clinician owns the stage; you do not name it.

# Pain at the heel attachment rather than mid-tendon

sub: Refer

Do this: The mid-tendon loading advice does not transfer to the attachment.

# She rolled it

sub: Refer

Do this: Then the ladder. Do not send her for an X-ray on your own initiative; a soft-tissue question is the clinician's call.

# She rolls it repeatedly, on curbs and steps

sub: Train the hip, not the ankle

Do this: If she is a recurring ankle sprain, the hips have to be super stable. Balancing a body on a house of cards does not work.

# "Bad ankle mobility" in a squat

sub: Run the heel-elevation test first

Do this: If depth appears, this is centre of mass and the wedge is the answer. Do not tell her it lengthened her calf.

# Ankle dumping inward on a step-down

sub: A physical block

Do this: Put your own foot where you do not want hers.

# A knee complaint with a stiff ankle

sub: The knee pages

Do this: The hinge adopts the rotation it cannot get above or below it.

# She wants to return to running or jumping

sub: The calf must move her own bodyweight

Do this: The protocol is in step 4.

# Calf or Achilles complaint plus a very rigid foot

sub: Work the foot

Do this: People with calf and Achilles issues tend to have very rigid feet.

Step 3. Do

The three steps, in order

Get the foot to move, and control centre of mass. Shoes off is a sensory-feedback tool, not a doctrine. That is what the barefoot calf raise on a block in Warming up is for: feel, not decompression.

Train the ankle: do calf raises. The seated, bent-knee raise specifically, because it pairs knee bend with ankle bend the way a squat does. Push the knees forward in the machine so the ankle is actually driven into the stretched position. Do it before squatting.

Add dynamic work. Holds with the heel floating, holds pushing against something immovable and holds resisting a load, at different angles. Then plenty of low-level plyometrics: getting better at managing ground contacts with some speed. The named beneficiary is the client who rolls her ankle on curbs.

What this replaces. Stretching and foam rolling all the time, without anything to strengthen or train better patterns in those positions, never reaches permanent improvement. It can help her access a position. It cannot be the whole intervention.

The foot-shape rule that decides the constraint. Knee forward in space means the foot rolls in and the shin rotates inward. Knee behind the ankle means the foot rolls out and the shin rotates outward.

Compared

columns: Stuck in a high arch, cannot roll in | Stuck flat, cannot roll out

Constraint: Knee-forward positions | Front foot elevated split squat, with the shin leaning back (negative shin angle)

Support: Heel wedge. A strap under the arch to bring the floor up to the arch | A strap under the arch to push it up

The limit on all of it. Constraints suggest she should succeed. They do not mean she always will.

Terms on this page

negative shin angle: Her front shin leans back, so the knee sits behind the ankle rather than out over the toes.
