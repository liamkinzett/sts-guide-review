Muscle and growth · T-01.18

What tells a muscle to grow?

A muscle grows because it is made to pull hard against a load. That pull is tension, and tension is the signal.

Muscle growth is an adaptation. When a muscle is repeatedly challenged by hard resistance training, the body changes the muscle so it can handle that demand again. One part of that change is an increase in the size of the muscle fibres themselves. That increase in fibre size is what a coach means by hypertrophy.

For the adaptation to start, the muscle needs a way to detect that it has been challenged. The main signal is mechanical tension (mechanical tension): the pulling force a muscle produces as it works against a load. On a hard leg extension, the quadriceps fibres doing the work have to pull against the resistance the machine provides. That pull, running through the working fibres, is the tension that matters. Exercise selection is built on this definition, and it is enough to coach with.

Turning that physical pull into a biological signal has its own name: mechanotransduction (mechanotransduction). Structures inside the fibre detect the mechanical stress and pass it on as a signal the cell can act on. Exactly which structures do the sensing is still being worked out. Two proteins are the likely candidates. A giant, spring-like protein (titin) is thought to unfold under load at long muscle lengths and switch itself on. A second structural protein (filamin) is thought to pass the signal along its own route.

Once the fibre has sensed the load, a switch inside it (mTORC1) becomes more active. More training volume turns that switch up further. The switch raises the rate at which the fibre builds new muscle protein, a process called muscle protein synthesis. Repeated sessions create repeated windows of building. When enough of that accumulates over weeks, the fibre gets bigger.

How the pull becomes muscle

The muscle is loaded. The fibres pull hard against the weight. That pull is the tension.

The fibre feels the pull. Titin and filamin are the likely sensors.

A switch turns on. The signal reaches mTORC1 inside the fibre.

Building rises. The switch raises the rate at which the fibre makes new protein. Over weeks, that is growth.

Which step is solid. Loading a muscle switching the building machinery on is not in question. Steps one, three and four are solid. Step two, the exact sensor that feels the pull, is the part still being investigated.

What this means for coaching. Nothing about how you program changes here. This does not hand you a new rule or a molecular pathway to chase in the gym. Its value is a better explanation for why resistance training works at all. Expose the relevant fibres to enough tension, repeatedly over time, then let the body adapt. What changes is your answer when a client asks how it works. Tell her plainly which step is settled, and which is still being worked out.

Say it like this

We know that loading a muscle switches the building on. Exactly how the fibre feels the pull is still being worked out. Either way, the load is what does it.

Terms on this page

mechanotransduction: The process of turning a physical stimulus, such as tension, into a biological signal inside the cell.

mechanical tension: The pulling force through a muscle as it works against a load. It is the main signal that tells a muscle to grow.

mTORC1: The switch inside a muscle fibre that turns mechanical loading into a higher rate of protein building.

titin: A giant, spring-like protein inside the fibre that carries passive tension. It is the likely sensor that feels the pull.

filamin: A structural protein in the fibre that is thought to pass the loading signal on to the building switch.
