What to do when she says she is exhausted

When she says she is exhausted, ask before you act. Her own report comes first, then her sleep, then her logbook. This page walks the whole topic in the order you use it, in the conversation where she says it.

Three look-alikes to route first

# She is sore, or worried that she is not sore

sub: Soreness measures nothing in either direction

Do this: Tell her soreness is not feedback. It is not exhaustion and it is not a sign of it. Read the logbook instead.

# She is flat at the same hour every week

sub: That is a fuelling pattern, not fatigue

Do this: Route it. Training around life covers the pattern, and anything about food is the Nutrition Manual's. Do not solve it here.

# She cramped mid-set

sub: The set ends. It is a setup question before a fuelling one

Do this: Check her setup on the machine before her hydration. Faults and what you can actually see holds the rule.

Test. Ask in this order.

Four questions before you change anything

Ask how she feels before she tells you her score. A client who reads her watch first feels what the number told her to feel. The wearable's number arrives second, as an input rather than a verdict.

Ask which exhausted she means. Foggy and not sharp is the nerve side (neurological fatigue). Muscles not working at their peak is the muscle side (metabolic fatigue). They recover on different timescales. A blanket deload answers one of them by accident. There is no gym test that separates them, so you ask her.

Ask about her sleep. One bad night measurably suppresses the adaptation. A client who is not sleeping is not absorbing the training.

Read her logbook. Soreness is not feedback. The logbook is.

Read. What the answers mean.

A client who is not sleeping is not absorbing the training. That is a programming input, not a wellbeing aside.

Falling numbers under continuous hard volume follow a known path. It is the client with no planned deloads.

It starts as a short overreach that a planned rest resolves (functional overreaching). Left alone, it becomes one that rest only returns the trainee to baseline from (non-functional overreaching). Past that it is a long collapse (overtraining syndrome).

The distinction that matters is time. The first resolves and leaves the trainee better. The other two do not. Do not call every ordinary dip in performance overtraining. Most bad weeks are not this.

Believe the performance drop is real rather than motivational. That is what a coach has to believe before she will deload someone who says she feels fine.

Local against systemic fatigue is a working read. Some tiredness sits in the muscle that did the work (local fatigue). Some sits in the whole body (systemic fatigue). Use the split, and judge it by eye, because nothing in the gym measures either.

Do not promise her a recovery timeline. There is no fixed curve for any tissue, any lift or any training age. Read her logbook and her report instead.

Do.

If sleep is the thing that is off, sleep is the thing you fix. You can write her more work. She will not get the benefit of it. This is also why you do not add volume to an under-slept client. Volume is fourth on the list when she stalls, behind asking whether she is training hard enough. Volume covers that order.

Do not gate the session on a wearable reading. Her report comes first. The score is one input.

One short night is not a reason to skip. Keep the session and tell her what to expect. The page on a single short night carries the detail. Do not invent a threshold of hours below which she stays home.

When she asks about a recovery tool

# A massage gun

sub: Allowed. Not recovery

Do this: Present it as something she may use, not as recovery. It does not cost her gains and it does not build any.

# A cold plunge

sub: Not close to training

Do this: Keep it well away from the session. Cold water straight after lifting costs her a little muscle. Timing is the lever, and nothing here bans cold water.

# Sauna

sub: No claim for it, no ban on it

Do this: Do not promote it as a muscle builder. If she enjoys it and it is not close to training, let it be.

# The paid recovery stack

sub: Priced far above what it delivers

Do this: Say so plainly. Redirect her to the unpriced things, led by sleep.

# Whether short rests cost her gains

sub: They do not, once the work is equal

Do this: Tell her the work is what matters. Rest and intensity techniques covers how rest periods are programmed.

Change.

Sleep enters the program as a training variable, not a lifestyle one. Volume does not go up while she is under-slept.

What explicitly does not change here. Her rest periods, which Rest and intensity techniques owns. Her food, which the Nutrition Manual owns. The one settled point about fuel is that it is usually not the limiter in lifting. And no deload is designed out of this topic. The phases owns deload design. This topic only tells you what she is deloading away from.

Escalate.

Where it goes when it is not this

# She looks under-fuelled

sub: Risk rises sharply where energy availability is low

Do this: The female body owns energy availability, and why the period is not a reliable screen for over half of your clients.

# She keeps getting sick inside one block

sub: Repeated illness is a training-response signal in a different costume

Do this: Before you call it bad luck, run the stall checklist in Volume the way you would for a lift that has stopped moving. Low energy availability shuts down immune function, and The female body owns that. Pain rules and when to refer holds the rule.

# It has become pain

sub: Pain is a different conversation

Do this: Pain rules and when to refer.

# A plateau to diagnose

sub: Her numbers have stopped, and she is not exhausted

Do this: Progression.

# She needs a deload designed

sub: You know what she is deloading from. Now design it

Do this: The phases.

# Life load rather than training load

sub: Work, family, travel, illness

Do this: Training around life.

# Not a training question at all

sub: Escalate rather than improvise

Do this: The coaching relationship.

How many rest days a week? There is no universal number, and she can still be given one. Rest days are what is left once frequency is set.

Frequency is arithmetic, not a target. Divide her weekly set band for a muscle by the per-session ceiling in Volume. That is how often the muscle needs training.

Split design then evolves the split with her capability, which moves how many training days the arithmetic asks for.

A beginner on full-body sessions may land on three. An advanced lifter splitting muscle groups may land on five or six, with the rest built into the split rather than scheduled beside it.

Refuse the universal number and do the arithmetic in front of her. A coach who says "it depends" and stops has not answered. A coach who shows the division has.

Terms on this page

neurological fatigue: The nerve side of being tired. The brain and nerves are not telling the muscles to do the right thing, and the person is not cognitively clear.

metabolic fatigue: The muscle side of being tired. The muscles themselves are not working at their peak.

functional overreaching: A short period of training past what the body can recover from, followed by a planned rest that leaves the trainee better than before.

non-functional overreaching: Training past recovery for long enough that the rest that follows only brings the trainee back to where they started.

overtraining syndrome: A long-lasting drop in performance and wellbeing after months of too much training and too little recovery. It takes months to resolve.

local fatigue: Tiredness in the muscle that did the work. It lowers that muscle's output and leaves the rest of her fresh.

systemic fatigue: Tiredness that affects the whole body, not one muscle. It shows as lower output across every lift and a flatter client.
