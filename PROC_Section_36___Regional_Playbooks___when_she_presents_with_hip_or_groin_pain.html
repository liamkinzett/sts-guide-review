What to do when she presents with hip or groin pain

Structure only indicates what a hip can do. Position dictates it. A hip you cannot change the structure of still has a position you can change, and that is where you start.

Work through it in this order

Test. Bodyweight squat, then all six directions, then the sort.

Read. Separate weakness, instability, tightness and lack of activation. Name a guess as a guess.

Do. Static stretch first, then stability.

Change. When load is not the dial, the stride is.

Escalate. Groin pain that does not respond, SI or pubic symphysis pain, and the general triggers.

Step 1. Test

The sequence, in order

Watch the bodyweight squat first. Knees clanking together, thighs rotating in, glutes not firing.

If the squat is clean, test the joint through all six directions, not the range the squat happens to demand.

Sort every movement into helps, no effect, hurts.

A clean squat rules out very little

Coaches use the range of the exercise as the diagnostic. The hip extends and rotates inward much further than a squat ever asks.

The commonest restriction is rotation, specifically internal rotation. That is the thigh turning inward in the hip socket (internal rotation). People over-mobilise the outward turn (external rotation) with pigeon and figure-four work and neglect the other direction entirely.

Step 2. Read

Four things get conflated here and they are not the same object. Weakness against strength. Instability against stability. Tightness against neither. Lack of activation, which causes instability and then reads as weakness. Deciding which you are looking at changes the intervention completely.

Name it as a guess, not as a finding. The read table below uses the proper clinical names. Say them as a guess, never grade them, and never let the guess delay a referral.

Step 3. Do

Static stretch first, then stability. Briefly quieting the tight tissue re-centres the joint. There is no point stabilising a joint being pulled in every direction at once. This is remedial work, not session prep, which is the distinction Warming up already draws.

Your working read. Name it, label it a guess

What you see, what you might guess, what changes on Monday

# Front of the hip, at the bottom of the squat or at the turnaround out of the hole

sub: Working read: anterior hip impingement

Why it happens: The provocative position is flexion plus adduction plus internal rotation: hip bent, thigh drawn in toward the midline (adduction), thigh turned in. Basically a bad squat. Soft tissue is pinched between the ball and the socket. Behind it: weakness on the outside, tightness on the inside (adductors, internal rotators, hip flexors), reinforced by a day sitting in a bent, drawn-in hip.

Do this: Cut depth to what she owns. Change stance. Then work the pattern behind it.

# The same, and it does not change with position or load

sub: Working read: femoroacetabular impingement (FAIS), bone shape doing the pinching

Do this: Refer. And do not let bone shape become the excuse for everything. Structure only indicates function. Position dictates it.

# Groin pain, standing cranked into extension to stay upright

sub: Working read: labrum or SI joint territory

Do this: Refer, and stop feeding extension. Restore hip flexion with internal rotation before loading the hinge again.

# She is missing hip extension

sub: She is likely also missing hip internal rotation

Why it happens: The two go together.

Do this: Do not chase them separately. The back leg of a split squat trains both.

# Pain building through a session, no single moment

sub: Working read: load tolerance, not a structure

Why it happens: Is this even an injury, or something cranky being rehabbed?

Do this: The sort. Take the provocative movement out, keep the session.

# A knee or a back that has not responded to knee-directed or back-directed work

sub: The hip

Why it happens: A man booked for a knee replacement measured minus 20 degrees of hip internal rotation, one of the worst measurements seen. Nobody had assessed anything but the knee, and he had had two failed interventions.

Do this: Screen the hip as standard on any lower-limb complaint, before you accept the region she named.

Which tests are yours to run. The Thomas test separates a one-joint hip flexor bias from a two-joint one. She lies on her back with one thigh hugged to her chest.

If the other knee rides up out of line with the hip, that reads as a one-joint bias. Straightening that knee puts the long thigh muscle (rectus femoris) under test.

Pair it with something upright: a lying test is so isolated that it needs an upright partner unless her whole plan happens on the floor. A loaded split squat compared side to side is that partner.

Findings without symptoms are common

Plenty of people with shoulder dislocations, ACL tears and hip labral injuries have no symptoms. A scan result is not the pain's cause, and a named read never outranks what she reports.

A working read never changes how it is said

It is a best guess, named with the proper clinical term, with a physio to confirm it. Never graded. Never a reason to delay a referral.

How a working read is said

My best guess, and it is a guess, not a diagnosis, is a pinch at the front of the hip in the bottom position. A physio confirms it. Meanwhile we change your stance and your depth and see what it does.

Step 4. Change

When load is not the dial, the stride is. A longer stride buys more flexion in the front hip and more extension in the trailing hip. Wider, stepping out at 45 degrees, works the adductors. Progressing that to a full lateral lunge is the most under-used tool for hip mobility, stability and strength.

Do not block the pelvis from translating sideways

The lateral shift IS the internal rotation. On a two-legged squat a shift is a fault to correct. On a step-up or split squat, ask first whether the shift is how she is producing force before you stop it.

Step 5. Escalate

Red flags

title: Refer, and change the plan

Groin pain that does not respond to load management

Pain at the joint where the spine meets the pelvis (SI joint), or at the front of the pelvis (pubic symphysis)

Everything in the general stop-and-refer list, plus seven out of ten reported consistently, plus pain still consistent between three days and one week

emergency: Do not send her for imaging early. Incidental findings become a diagnosis she suffers from. A clinician's six-to-eight-week window is theirs and does not govern you: the two numbers decide when you escalate.

Terms on this page

FAIS: Femoroacetabular impingement syndrome. Pinching at the front of the hip caused by the shape of the bone itself, either a bump on the ball or an over-deep socket, so the ball catches on the front rim of the socket. A physio or sports physician names it.

rectus femoris: The one quad muscle that crosses the hip as well as the knee, running down the front of the thigh. Straightening the knee during a Thomas test puts it under tension.

SI joint: The sacroiliac joint, where the base of the spine meets the pelvis on each side, near the dimples of the low back.

pubic symphysis: The joint at the very front of the pelvis where the two pubic bones meet. Pain here is common in pregnancy and postpartum.

internal rotation: Turning a limb inward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face back or down, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more toward the middle. The muscles that do it are the internal rotators.

external rotation: Turning a limb outward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face forward or up, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more to the side. A muscle that does it is an external rotator.

adduction: A limb travelling in toward the midline of the body: the leg pulled in by the inner-thigh muscles, or the arm brought in to the side.
