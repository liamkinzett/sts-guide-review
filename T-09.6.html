Verify her reps in reserve before you build on them

Check her reps in reserve before you program off them. Trained lifters rate their reps in reserve (RIR) accurately. Less experienced lifters do not. They miss in a consistent direction: they believe they are closer to failure than they are.

Assessing a new client owns the rule. This page is the toolkit. Three methods, in ascending order of how much they tell you.

Three ways to check

# The rest test

sub: Sixty seconds, then the same set again

What you do: Have her stop a set she calls one rep short. She rests sixty seconds, then repeats it at the same weight.

What it tells you: If she comfortably does, she was not where she thought she was. Assessing a new client has the full test.

# The video audit

sub: The speed of the last reps

What you do: Compare the last two reps of a set against the first two. The next page has the detail.

What it tells you: Whether the set was close, on target, or past it. It cannot be faked.

# The logbook over time

sub: Loads against reported effort

What you do: Watch what her reported reps in reserve does while her loads climb.

What it tells you: Steady load gains with a reported RIR that never moves means one of two things. Either she is calibrating as she gets stronger, which is what you want. Or she has settled on a number she reports rather than a number she observes.

Why it matters. Every load, every rep band and every progression decision sits on her effort number. If the number is wrong, everything built on it is wrong. A logbook that says 2 RIR when the truth is 5 RIR describes a client who is not training.

Watch for

Asking her whether she trained hard. She will say yes. She will believe it. Every method above works because it does not rely on her answer.

Terms on this page

RIR: Reps in reserve. How many more reps she could have done when the set stopped. Zero RIR is failure.
