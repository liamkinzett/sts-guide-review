What to do when she presents with low back pain

Low back pain is the region where an ordinary-looking presentation can hide an emergency. So the order matters. You screen first, read second, and only then change anything.

Two things this page does not let you do. You do not sort her pain into structural, functional or stress-driven, and you do not grade it minor or major. Both are a clinician's diagnosis. What you may do is name your best guess, label it a guess, change what you train, and refer. The pages on the three buckets exist so you understand what the people who do diagnose are thinking about.

Work through it in this order

Test. Screen for the red flags before anything else. Two of them end the session.

Read. Sort pain from discomfort. Take the history: what changed? Apply the two numbers that escalate a case.

Do. Sort every movement into three buckets and build her program from what does not hurt. Name your working read, as a guess.

Change. Read progress off her day, not off the bar. She will feel good before she is good.

Escalate. Anything acute refers. Anything still consistent between three days and one week escalates. Known osteoporosis is a hard limit.

Step 1. Test

Screen before anything else, in this order. Two of these end the session.

Red flags

title: Stop and refer if she has any of these

Any sign of pressure on the nerves at the base of the spine (cauda equina syndrome). Emergency department, today. It does not wait for an appointment.

Pain running down the leg (radicular pain), tingling, numbness or weakness in the leg. Stop and refer to a physiotherapist.

She becomes lightheaded on a hinge. Stop the movement and refer. It is a health sign, not a technique fault.

emergency: The cauda equina signs are new bladder trouble, numbness around the saddle area, or loss of the feeling of a full bowel. Also new sexual dysfunction, or severe or worsening leg weakness. Any of these with back or leg pain, starting in the last two weeks: emergency department the same day.

Lightheaded on a hinge

Do not coach it as a bracing fault. The plausible causes are the inner-ear balance system (vestibular) or her blood pressure. Neither is yours to fix. Stop the movement and refer.

None of the above? Continue to step 2.

Step 2. Read

The first question is never how bad. It is which of two things it is. Discomfort is the set working: it burns, and continuing produces an adaptation. Pain is a warning: continuing produces a worse outcome. Intensity does not separate them. Direction does.

Then ask what changed before you change anything. Was there an incident, or did it creep in? Did one rep feel different from the one before? Has training, recovery or life changed recently? Is she in a deficit? Take every previous injury, not just this one.

Then the two numbers that escalate a case. Seven out of ten, reported consistently, escalates. Still consistent between three days and one week escalates. Both mean a referral, whatever your read says.

Step 3. Do

Sort every movement, position and daily activity into three buckets. What makes it feel better, what changes nothing, and what makes it worse. The first two buckets are her program. Not a lighter version of her program: her actual program. The third bucket is ranked from most tolerable to least, and that ranking is the order movements come back.

That sorting is yours. It needs no diagnosis. It is a different thing from the three diagnostic buckets on the other low back pages, which belong to clinicians.

Where the presentation is one we already know, the first lever is written down.

Three presentations with a known first lever

# Low-back pump on an RDL

sub: Her lower back fills up and burns during Romanian deadlifts (RDL)

Why it happens: It is a clue, not a verdict. Four causes, checked in this order: pressure heavy in her heels, hinging through the knee, going past her active hip range, and losing her brace.

Do this: Work down the four causes in order. Fix the first one you find, then look again.

# "Feeling it in the low back" on a hip extension or kickback

sub: She reports it. You will not see it

Why it happens: Her lower back is supplying range that her hip should be supplying.

Do this: Ask her after the set, every time. This is a fault you find by asking, never by watching.

# Position degrading across the set

sub: Rep one looks nothing like rep eight

Why it happens: She has run out of the strength or the brace to hold the position under that load.

Do this: Reduce the load.

Your working read. Name it, label it a guess

Gym-onset low back pain splits three ways: a strain or sprain, a disc herniation, or degeneration. Most of what we see is strain or sprain. Very tight muscles, protecting something.

What you see, what you might guess, what changes on Monday

# Ache across the low back after heavier hinging. No leg symptoms. Tight and guarded

sub: Working read: a strain or sprain

Why it happens: Four likely sites. The spinal erectors. The deep muscle at the side of the lower back (quadratus lumborum). The ligaments of the joint where spine meets pelvis (SI joint). The ligaments of the lower back itself (lumbar).

Do this: Run the three-bucket sort. Drop the horizontal hinge and keep the session.

# One side, near the dimple, worst on stiff-leg deadlifts and RDLs

sub: Working read: the SI joint

Why it happens: The SI joint is the most common low back presentation we see. Heavier lifting, mainly hinging, is what aggravates it. Bending over and pushing the hips back loads it more.

Do this: Move down the torso-inclination spectrum. Trade the RDL for a more upright pattern rather than banning the hinge. SI pain is also a referral in its own right: see the last row.

# She cannot hold ribs stacked over pelvis under load. Arched through the low back, the Coke-can-crushed shape

sub: Working read: force is going through structure, not muscle

Why it happens: She is hiding the force in bone and ligament instead of muscle. That is why the SI joint hurts.

Do this: This is a position problem. Fix the stack before you touch load.

# Pain travelling below the knee, or numbness, pins and needles or weakness

sub: Working read: a nerve root (radicular pain), possibly a (disc herniation)

Why it happens: Something is pressing on a nerve where it leaves the spine.

Do this: Refer, today. Do not sort, do not test further. Emergency department if there is any bowel or bladder change.

# A scan naming a "slipped disc" or similar, in someone already in pain

sub: Working read: an over-reached label

Why it happens: People start hurting, get a scan, and the scan shows something. It could have been there before the pain ever started.

Do this: Do not let the label drive the program. A disc herniation is not permanent and it is not career-ending.

# Pregnant or postpartum, or pubic symphysis pain, SI pain, sciatica, any leaking or any pelvic floor symptom

sub: Outside this topic. See The female body

Why it happens: These belong with a pelvic health physiotherapist. That covers pain at the front of the pelvis (pubic symphysis), pain from the buttock down the leg (sciatica), and any (pelvic floor) symptom.

Do this: Refer out. If the only referral available cannot load a client, collaborate with them rather than hand her off entirely.

How a working read is said

My best guess from what I'm seeing, and it is a guess, not a diagnosis, is a strain around the SI joint. A physio is the one who confirms it. Here is what I'd change in the meantime.

Insufficient on their own while the provoking pattern stays in the program

Bird dog, curl-ups, the (McGill big three), the (McKenzie method), side-posture adjustment, and soft-tissue release on the QL (ART). None is condemned as an exercise. All are condemned as insufficient while the provocative loading continues. Change the loading first.

A working read never changes how it is said

It is a best guess, named with the proper clinical term, with a physio to confirm it. Never graded. Never a reason to delay a referral. Holding a read is not the same as stating a diagnosis.

Step 4. Change

Read progress off her day, not off the bar. Stairs, getting out of the car, rolling over in bed, putting her socks on. Those move first. Load is the last thing to improve, and you have to re-ask about the daily signs because she will not volunteer them.

She will feel good before she is good. One good session is not permission. The load comes back slower than her confidence does, so make the program cap the load rather than her intent.

Explaining is treatment here, not admin

Tell her what provokes it and what relieves it, out loud. In the low back, stress and symptom feed each other. Showing her that you understand her pain is treatment of that loop.

Step 5. Escalate

Anything acute refers. Something went wrong, fast, in one moment: she gets checked. Anything still consistent between three days and one week escalates to a referral. Do not judge severity in the first twenty-four hours either way. She can feel fine the next day and be worse on day three.

The hard limit. In known thinning of the vertebrae (vertebral osteoporosis), loaded forward flexion and rapid explosive spinal twisting are contraindicated. No deviation.

Watch your language while you do all of this

People begin suffering from a diagnosis. The lumbar disc is the standing example. Talk about what she can do, not about the label.

That is a caution about how you talk. It is never a reason to delay a referral the emergency signs or the stop-and-refer triggers require.

Terms on this page

cauda equina syndrome: The bundle of nerves at the base of the spine is being squashed. Early signs are bladder or bowel changes, numbness around the saddle area, new sexual dysfunction or worsening leg weakness.

radicular pain: Pain that runs down the leg along the path of a nerve, often with tingling, numbness or weakness. It means a nerve root is being irritated where it leaves the spine.

vestibular: The balance system in the inner ear. When it misfires she feels dizzy or lightheaded on a change of head position.

RDL: The Romanian deadlift. A hinge from standing with a slight knee bend, pushing the hips back, that loads the hamstrings and glutes.

quadratus lumborum: The deep muscle at the side of the lower back, running between the pelvis and the lowest rib. Coaches call it the QL. It is a common site of low back strain.

SI joint: The sacroiliac joint, where the base of the spine meets the pelvis on each side, near the dimples of the low back.

disc herniation: The soft centre of a spinal disc pushes out through its outer ring and can press on a nerve. It can cause pain down the leg. It is not permanent and it is not career-ending.

pubic symphysis: The joint at the very front of the pelvis where the two pubic bones meet. Pain here is common in pregnancy and postpartum.

sciatica: Pain that runs from the buttock down the back of the leg along the sciatic nerve. It is a form of radicular pain.

pelvic floor: The sling of muscle at the base of the pelvis that supports the bladder, bowel and womb. Leaking, heaviness or dragging are pelvic floor symptoms.

McGill big three: A set of three low back exercises: the curl-up, the side plank and the bird dog. Used to build trunk endurance without moving the spine.

McKenzie method: A physiotherapy approach to back pain that uses repeated movements, often extension, to find a direction that eases symptoms.

ART: Active Release Technique. A hands-on soft-tissue treatment where the practitioner presses on a muscle while it is moved through its range.

vertebral osteoporosis: The bones of the spine have lost density and can fracture under loads a healthy spine would tolerate. A coach who suspects it refers, and never loads forward flexion or fast twisting.

lumbar: The lower back: the part of the spine between the ribs and the pelvis.
