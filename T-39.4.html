Reading her training data · T-39.4

Change one thing. Change the smallest thing that could work

When the diagnosis says something must change, change one thing, and make it the smallest thing that could work. This is the usual choice, and there is one reason to depart from it.

Smallest to largest

A cue. Cueing owns it. Tested in one set.

A setup or a constraint. A heel wedge, a stance, a lighter version. Cueing and Diagnosing from video own it.

Load. Effort and intensity owns it.

Range. Tempo and pauses and the exercise pages own it.

Volume. Volume owns it, and it is fourth on Volume's own diagnosis list.

The exercise. Choosing exercises owns it.

The split. Split design owns it.

Why this order. Each rung down the list costs more to reverse and takes longer to read. A cue can be tested in one set. A load change shows in a session. A split change cannot be evaluated for a month, and while you wait you have lost the ability to read anything else.

Where you depart from it. Deviate where two changes are genuinely coupled, such as reducing load while lengthening a range. Then make both, and say so in the notes, so the next reader knows the pair was deliberate.

A worked read. Her Romanian deadlift has been flat for two exposures. The diagnosis says her effort is fine, her recovery is fine, and her range has shortened. The smallest change is a cue for depth. If the cue does not hold, a constraint: a lighter version or a block to reach to. Load, volume and the exercise itself stay where they are until those have been read.

Watch for

Reaching down the list because the bigger change feels more like coaching. The bigger change is the one you cannot read for a month.
