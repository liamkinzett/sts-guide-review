Hip: when to escalate, and the imaging warning

Conservative management first, always. A clinician's window for that is six to eight weeks. Your escalation clock is shorter, and it is not theirs.

The fishnet. The stretch-then-stabilise cluster catches most hip presentations. What swims through is bone-shape pinching at the front of the hip (FAIS). That is what a clinician images for, after six to eight weeks of conservative work has not moved it. Real tissue and movement-pattern change takes that long.

Verdict

That six-to-eight-week window is a clinician's, and it does not govern you.

Pain still consistent between three days and one week escalates to a referral. Holding a persistent hip for six weeks is what a clinician does with a client they have already assessed.

The warning against imaging early is incidental findings. Don't go down that road if you are not willing to do something about it. People start suffering from a diagnosis, with a disc in the lower back (lumbar) as the standing example. A scan finds things in pain-free people.

This is a caution about how you talk

It is never a reason to delay a referral the red flags or the stop-and-refer triggers require. You do not order imaging and you do not talk her out of it. You refer, and you keep the label out of the program.

Terms on this page

FAIS: Femoroacetabular impingement syndrome. Pinching at the front of the hip caused by the shape of the bone itself, either a bump on the ball or an over-deep socket, so the ball catches on the front rim of the socket. A physio or sports physician names it.

lumbar: The lower back: the part of the spine between the ribs and the pelvis.
