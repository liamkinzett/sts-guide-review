Chest · E-CHE-DBPRESS#Role

What it's for

The dumbbell bench press is the horizontal press with a free, converging arm path. STS prefers it to the barbell for chest growth on three grounds. The hands travel a natural converging path. The arms are free to rotate. The range is not fixed by a rigid bar.

The dumbbell is the chest-growth default, and the reason is not more chest activation. The pec and the front delt work about the same on a barbell and on dumbbells. What differs is the triceps. It is considerably more active on the barbell.

A fixed bar lets her press the load through the triceps in a way two independent hands cannot. On dumbbells the triceps give out later, so more of the set is limited by the chest. That is the reason.

Do not tell a coach the dumbbell "hits the chest harder". It does not. The barbell also takes roughly 20% heavier loads, which is why it stays the strength-path pick.

The resistance rises as she lowers (ascending resistance). It is hardest at the bottom.

It loads the stretched and mid positions well and gives nothing at the squeezed end. That is why a pec fly is placed after the presses rather than instead of them.

Bench angle is a line-of-force variable, not a cosmetic one. A low incline, which is the 30 degree bench, biases the upper chest (clavicular fibres). Flat and decline arm paths bias the middle and lower chest (sternal and costal fibres). Record the angle numerically so the exercise is repeatable rather than approximately repeated.

Which angle, for which client: structure sets it. Assess her rib-cage shape and how her chest expands before you assign a pressing angle. A higher angle shifts the line of force onto the front deltoid, so a low incline is the usual staple. There is no universal angle. Choose it for the client and record it. Never promise her that one angle grows one part of the chest more than another.

The two incline angles are consecutive rungs, not alternatives. Where a client cannot yet press overhead, run them as an ordered progression. Start with a cable press in a more neutral plane. Then maybe move to a 30 degree incline dumbbell press. From the 30 degree incline, maybe move to the 45. Eventually she is getting into something overhead.

The angle is selected on what her shoulder currently tolerates. It climbs from there. Shoulders runs the same ladder in the other direction for an injured shoulder.

Why the supported incline works. A low incline bench makes the stacked rib position compulsory. Try to hold a sternum-down slump on one and gravity is going to win. Gravity opens the chest for a client who has no awareness to open it herself. That makes the bench a teaching constraint, not only a loading choice. Raising the incline over time is a measuring stick of progression, until she can go seated overhead.

Its slot. The primary chest press in a physique-goal program. It is also the named accessory that restores shoulder-blade freedom for a client whose main pressing is a locked-down barbell bench.

Terms on this page

ascending resistance: The load feels heavier the further into the movement she goes. On a dumbbell press that is the bottom, half an inch off the chest.

clavicular fibres: The upper part of the chest muscle, running from the collarbone to the upper arm. An incline biases them.

sternal and costal fibres: The middle and lower parts of the chest muscle, running from the breastbone and ribs to the upper arm. A flat or decline arm path biases them.
