What does specificity actually mean?

When you pick an exercise, ask how closely it trains the adaptation you want. Not how closely it looks like the task. That question is specificity (specificity), and it sits at the top of the selection hierarchy.

Specificity is often misunderstood as visual resemblance. A coach sees a target task, picks another exercise that looks similar, and calls it specific. Sometimes that works. Often it does not. The better question is what quality is limiting the outcome, what adaptation is required, and which exercise builds that adaptation most effectively. An exercise can look less like the goal while being more specific to the missing adaptation.

Make the things that build the quality as good as they can be, even when they look less specific. That is the most specific way to train the quality. The named error is choosing a movement that resembles the goal while delivering a weaker stimulus for the quality underneath. The blunt version is mimicry: shooting free throws with a medicine ball.

For a woman who wants bigger glutes, the question is not which exercise looks most like the goal. It is which movement loads the glute hardest.

Transfer is rarely all or nothing. An exercise variation carries over well to another movement when the tasks are similar in what matters:

  • the joint actions involved;
  • the range of motion;
  • the force demands;
  • the positions trained;
  • the coordination pattern;
  • and the muscles doing the work.

    • Transfer gets weaker as the variation becomes less similar in what the nervous system actually has to solve. A beginner can gain from almost any reasonably chosen variation, because there is so much room to improve. A more advanced lifter usually needs greater specificity if the target lift itself has to move.

    Strength is also partly a skill. Repeating the same movement teaches a lifter:

  • how to organise the pattern more efficiently;
  • how to brace for it;
  • how to time force output;
  • how to coordinate the joints and muscles involved;
  • and how to stay stable in the positions that matter.

    • That is one reason someone can get stronger at a squat by squatting more skilfully, even before a large change in muscle size. It is also why gaining muscle in a muscle group does not automatically show up as the full possible improvement in a specific lift. That happens if the skill of that lift is not practised.

    Why it matters. Resemblance is the intuitive reading of "specific", and it is the wrong one. It produces exercises that look purposeful and train nothing well. Use variation strategically, not romantically. It earns its place when it:

  • builds the missing adaptation;
  • reduces a problem;
  • exposes a weak region;
  • or allows quality work the target lift cannot currently provide.

    • It earns nothing just because it looks specific.

    Watch for

    A movement justified by what it resembles. Ask what quality it is meant to build. Then ask whether anything builds that quality better.

    Say it like this

    This doesn't look like what you're training for. It builds the thing that's actually holding you back, which is why it's the more specific choice.

    Terms on this page

    specificity: The closer the training is to the quality or lift she wants to improve, the more it carries over. Choose the exercise by the adaptation it produces rather than by what it looks like.
