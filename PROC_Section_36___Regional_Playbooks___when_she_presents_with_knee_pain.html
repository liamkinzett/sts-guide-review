What to do when she presents with knee pain

The knee is rarely the problem. It is where missing hip rotation, missing pelvic stability and missing ankle motion land. Knee-directed work is the symptom-treating option.

Work through it in this order

Test. The sort, the load-matching test, the lying foot check.

Read. The whole injury history, then pain or discomfort, then the two numbers.

Do. Reduce the visible left-right difference. Name your working read, as a guess.

Change. Work the hip and the ankle, then re-run the load-matching test.

Escalate. Anything acute refers. Three presentations leave the gym today.

Step 1. Test

Three things. None of them is a diagnosis.

The three tests

# The sort

sub: Every movement into helps, no effect, hurts

Do this: The sort is the plan, not a step toward one.

# The load-matching test

sub: The same movement at the same load on both sides

Do this: A quad-biased split squat with a five second lowering phase. The difference in shaking and burn is the data.

# The lying foot check

sub: She lies down on a mat. Look at her feet at rest

Do this: One foot splayed out while the other points up is high-value information. Resting tone is how the nervous system chose to create stability.

Step 2. Read

Take the whole injury history before you change anything. Pain that changed after another injury usually points at the ankle or the hip rather than the knee. Then which of two things it is, pain or discomfort. Then the two numbers that escalate: seven out of ten reported consistently, or still consistent between three days and one week.

Step 3. Do

Reduce the visible left-right difference. You do not need to know why. A lot of the time it does not matter why she has it. Equalise before you progress.

First lever by presentation

# Pain on any loaded knee bend

sub: The sort is the plan

Do this: Train what helps and what does nothing.

# Knee pain on extensions

sub: Ask. You will not see it

Do this: Ask after the set, then run the pain rules. Quads carries the leg extension's one injury-grade fault.

# Pain at depth in a squat

sub: Depth is what she owns under load

Do this: Quads covers it.

# Pain that changed after another injury

sub: The ankle or the hip is the likelier origin

Do this: Take the whole history.

# The knee travelling inside the foot

sub: Depth decides

Do this: See the page on knees falling inward (knee valgus). Above 90 degrees it is a fault. Below parallel it is not.

Your working read. Name it, label it a guess

First fork, and it decides everything after it: structure or function? There is a lot that can go wrong in the knee and not many ways to fix it at the knee directly.

What sits here
StructureACL, MCL, LCL, the two menisci, the patellar tendon, the quad tendon, a fracture of the kneecap or the end of the thigh bone
FunctionHamstring, calf, quad, IT band
Red flags

title: Three that leave the gym today, not after the session

The extensor mechanism. Lift her foot for her while she sits. If that is painless, ask her to hold it there, then let go. If the leg drops like a stone it is clinical. A quad tendon rupture belongs on a table inside 24 hours, and the reflex to avoid surgery is the error here

Mechanism. Acute, traumatic, impact: refer today, and the clinician images before anything else. Transient or long-standing: do not push her toward a scan

Locking or the knee giving way, with joint-line pain in deep knee bend, together. Refer

emergency: The two numbers and the red flags are untouched by any of this.

Then the named reads. Say it as a guess and send her to a physio to confirm it.

What you see, what you might guess, what changes on Monday

# Locking, or the knee giving way, plus joint-line pain in deep knee bend

sub: Working read: meniscus

Why it happens: The two halves of the tell together.

Do this: Refer. And hold the population in mind: a torn meniscus past 60 is like wrinkled skin. Many clients have one and are pain free once they move well. A scan finding is not the pain's cause.

# Pain on both squeezing the quad and stretching it, at the front of the knee, no instability

sub: Working read: the quad or patellar tendon, not a ligament

Why it happens: Muscle and tendon hurt both when they work and when they are stretched. A ligament does not.

Do this: Tissue that contracts responds to loading. Holds below the painful angle first.

# Front-of-knee pain in a lifter who cannot rotate the thigh through deep bend with the foot fixed

sub: Working read: patellar tendinopathy

Why it happens: In powerlifters, roughly 70/30 toward a lack of hip internal rotation, the thigh turning inward in its socket (internal rotation). The knee is where the twist lands, not where it starts.

Do this: Train hip internal rotation.

# Pain going down stairs, not up, and not during training

sub: Working read: a lowering-tolerance problem, not a strength one

Why it happens: Descending is deceleration. The quad is lengthening under load.

Do this: Load the lowering phase deliberately: step-downs and slow split squats.

# Any knee with a previous ACL reconstruction

sub: Working read: a ligament that does not fully return

Why it happens: The ACL resists the shin sliding forward. So do the hamstrings, so they are the functional substitute.

Do this: Program the hamstrings for that job. End-range knee bend may never come back if it is not addressed early. Take it too far too soon and the new ACL is wrecked. Say both halves in the same breath.

# A non-contact injury: a cut sideways, a knee collapsing inward, late in a session

sub: Working read: an acute injury from a long-standing stressor

Why it happens: A hamstring too fatigued to resist the shin sliding forward leaves the ACL to do it alone.

Do this: Program the specific position she will be re-exposed to. Do not spend her hamstring reserve the day before it is needed.

The stairs reading stands. The lowering phase is the thing to load. The step-down and the slow split squat both carry that job and both are in the Library.

Two things are barred

You do not grade it. No "mild", no "grade 2".

You do not run the hands-on integrity tests clinicians use. Anterior drawer and Lachman are clinicians' tests. Everything above is something she reports or something you watch.

How you hand it on: the SOAP note. What she said, what you measured, what you make of it, what you plan.

The worked example: right knee pain, worse up and down stairs. Two sets of box step-ups on a 12-inch box, 10 reps on the left and only 6 on the right. 45 lb on a leg extension on the right against 85 on the left.

Assessment: a strength gap right against left, with pain in deep knee bend. Plan: holds at a joint angle below where she feels it, limited range, then dynamic work, building capacity over three months.

You are adding depth to their diagnosis, not competing with it.

How a working read is said

My best guess, and it is a guess, not a diagnosis, is the patellar tendon. A physio confirms it. Meanwhile we work your hip rotation and hold below the angle that hurts.

Step 4. Change

Resist the pull toward the joint itself

Terminal knee extensions and IT band rolling are the symptom-treating option. Work the hip and the ankle, then re-run the load-matching test to see whether the difference moved.

Read progress off her day rather than off the bar. She will feel good before she is good.

Step 5. Escalate

Red flags

title: Refer, and change the plan

Anything acute

Pain plus swelling, locking, clicking or popping

Everything in the general stop-and-refer list, plus seven out of ten reported consistently, plus pain still consistent between three days and one week

emergency: A leg that drops like a stone when she tries to hold it straight is a quad tendon rupture until proven otherwise. Inside 24 hours.

One loading range this region singles out. A knee bent to between 80 and 100 degrees carries the highest stress on the kneecap. Stopping at half depth is not a neutral place to stop.

Do not read a pain-free session as the problem solved

The dysfunction was there before the pain and it persists after it. Not being in pain does not mean she has a high level of function.

Terms on this page

internal rotation: Turning a limb inward in its socket: the upper arm at the shoulder, so the palm turns to face back or down, or the thigh at the hip, so the knee points more toward the middle. The muscles that do it are the internal rotators.

knee valgus: Her knees fall inwards, inside the line of her toes, as she squats or stands up.
