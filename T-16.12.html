Warming up · T-16.12

Static stretching before training: under sixty seconds only

A stretch held under 60 seconds may be used before or during a session. A stretch held 60 seconds or longer may not. The threshold is the rule.

Key figure

value: under 60

unit: seconds per static hold before training

note: Sixty and over makes her weaker for the session. PNF sits outside this rule.

Why it works. Stretch-induced strength loss is real. It comes from a mechanical drop in the stiffness of the muscle and tendon plus a neural drop in drive. The loss depends on how long she holds, and it is not all or nothing.

Under 30 seconds the measured cost is about 1.1%. At 30 to 45 seconds it is about 1.9%. Both sit inside the noise of a training session. At 60 seconds and over the loss becomes real, and she is genuinely weaker for the session she is about to do.

PNF and contract-relax sit outside this rule entirely, at any duration. She takes the joint to end range, contracts against resistance, then relaxes into the new range. The contraction is the test. It is a different thing from a passive hold. Tightness is often the nervous system protecting a range, and a PNF regains that range so it can be loaded straight away. Loading it is what keeps it, which Mobility and stretching explains.

This is still not a rule about stretching. It is a rule about how long. Passing it means the stretch will not cost her the session. It does not put the stretch in the prep slot. Mobility and stretching owns what to tell a client who asks for a stretching routine.

Watch for

The hold of 90 seconds. It is the most common prescription in published mobility routines, and it is on the far side of the threshold.

The coach who reads this as permission and parks a client in a five-minute stretching block before a session.

Say it like this

Short holds before lifting are fine, they cost you nothing. Anything you are settling into for a minute or more, save it for after.

Terms on this page

PNF: A stretch where she takes the joint to end range, pushes against resistance for a few seconds, then relaxes into the new range. The contraction is what makes it PNF.
