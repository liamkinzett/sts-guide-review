Faults and what you can actually see · T-32.2

Four tiers decide whether a fault can be screened at all

Before you name a fault, know its tier. The tier says how you find it: watch for it, film a particular angle, ask about it, or audit your own program.

The four tiers

# Tier 1: invisible in principle

sub: Not a fault. Never written as an observation

What it is: A change too small to see, or something that needs hands on her to measure.

What you do: Nothing. It is not screened and it is never recorded as something you saw.

# Tier 2: visible only from a specific angle

sub: A fault only if the film shows that angle

What it is: A real, visible event that one camera position shows and another hides.

What you do: Call it present or absent only when the footage could have shown it. Otherwise it is unknown, not absent.

# Tier 3: real, found by report or protocol

sub: You ask. It is not a video finding

What it is: Something she feels, or something a test surfaces. It never appears on screen.

What you do: Ask her. Or run the protocol.

# Tier P: in the prescription, not the rep

sub: You audit your own program

What it is: A fault you committed when you wrote the program. There is nobody to ask and nothing to watch.

What you do: Read the program, not the client. A Nordic prescribed at too low a frequency. A seat height wrong for the target. Dumbbells used for flyes. A box too high for her hips. A pulley set high on a lengthened cable curl.

Where the tier lives on an exercise page. Each fault row on an exercise page has an "On video" column. A filled cell describing something you can see is the statement that the fault is screenable. The tier is written out only when the answer is not "watch it": Tier 1, Tier 3 or Tier P. A row with a filled "On video" cell and no tier is screenable, and that is not an omission.

Why it works. Each tier answers a different failure. Tier 1 stops a coach inventing an observation. Tier 2 stops her recording an absence she had no way to see. Tier 3 stops her watching for something that was never going to appear on screen. Tier P stops her looking at the client for a fault she committed herself.

Tier P is a letter, not a number, on purpose. A fourth number would read as the far end of a severity scale. P is for program. It sits outside the ordering because it is the only tier where the fault is not the client's.

Watch for

Treating the tier as a severity ranking. It is not. A Tier 3 fault can end a set immediately. The tier says how you find it, not how much it matters.
