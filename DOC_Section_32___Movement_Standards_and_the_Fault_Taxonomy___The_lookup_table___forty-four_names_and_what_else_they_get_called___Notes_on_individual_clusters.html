Notes on individual faults in the table

Six rows in the lookup table need more than a name. Read these before you use them.

Notes on individual faults

# Row 24: straps are a fault only while grip is part of the demand

sub: Ask what the rate limiter is meant to be

The rule: "Using straps" is listed as a fault, and that is right only while grip is part of the training target. Where the set exists to train the back, a grip that fails first has ended the set early rather than made it harder. By the third rep she should not be thinking about whether the bar is slipping. Then she is not thinking about how she is moving.

Where straps sit: Permitted on the free-weight row family. Refused on the pull-up, where grip is part of the demand. On a hanging leg raise, straps or the captain's chair when grip ends the set before the abs do. On an RDL, a neutral grip first, and straps when grip limits the set.

Sweaty hands: Liquid chalk, chosen over powder because it is contained and because quite a few gyms have banned loose chalk.

# Row 3: the name travels, the fixes do not

sub: Write row 3's name, then take the fix from the exercise page

The rule: "The range appears at a neighbouring joint" absorbs hinging through the knee on an RDL and hinging instead of curling. As a mechanism that holds: the range shows up at a joint that was not the target. The fixes do not generalise. "Spine long", "top of the head to the ceiling" and "stay on the pad" are all about the upper back on a rowing pattern. Cue "spine long" at a client hinging through the knee and you have addressed nothing.

Do this: The cluster name answers what the fault is called. The exercise page answers what to do about it.

# Row 17: butt wink depends on the implement

sub: Free squat: find what her wink responds to. Back-supported machine: stop above it

The rule: On a free squat the pelvis tucks at depth with no pad involved, and whether that matters depends on the client. On a hack squat, pendulum squat or leg press, the pelvis peeling off the pad means the lower back loses its support. The machine keeps driving load through it. Stop above it. The instruction there is unambiguous because the mechanics are.

Do this: The butt wink procedure lives in Quads, on the barbell squat page. It works by finding what this client's wink responds to, and it needs no verdict on whether a wink is a fault.

# Row 2: "feeling it in the low back" is a report, not an observation

sub: Write it as "Lumbar finish (Tier 3, client report)"

The rule: It is the only alias in the whole table that a client says rather than a coach sees. The name is right: a finish that comes from the lower back (lumbar) is the same fault. But the record must not claim you watched something she told you. A canonical name never overwrites a tier.

# Row 21: knee travel inward depends on depth, and on her build

sub: Above 90 degrees a genuine fault. Below parallel, often not

The rule: Small side-to-side knee travel is not screenable by eye. Beyond that, depth decides. Knees caving above 90 degrees, before she reaches depth, is a genuine fault. Knees travelling in below parallel, hip crease under the knee, is loading the hip stabilisers. And a woman's neutral knee position sits further in than a man's (Q angle), so a male alignment standard applied to her manufactures a fault. Region by region carries the knee in full.

# Rows 16, 19, 22, 23 and 28: judge in context

sub: Whether it is a fault depends on the movement and the client

The rule: All five are real faults on most movements. The pelvis escaping to the back leg, weight into the heels, knees driven out too hard. Base too wide or too long, and an undeclared scapular bias. On some setups the same position is the intended one, and on some clients it is how she produces force. A hip shift may be how she stands up; a wide base may be her stance. Ask what the exercise is for before you call it.

Watch for

Taking a fix from the lookup table. It holds names. Fixes live on the exercise page.

Correcting a hip shift before asking whether it is how she produces force. Region by region covers when a shift matters.

Terms on this page

Q angle: The angle between the line of the thigh and the line of the shin at the knee. A wider pelvis gives a larger angle, so the knee sits further inside the hip.

lumbar: The lower back: the part of the spine between the ribs and the pelvis. When a lift "goes to her lower back", this is where.

scapular: To do with the shoulder blade, the scapula. Scapular stability is the shoulder blade holding still while the arm works.
